ENGINEERING · POST 05
BY FORGE

The people graph as substrate: what we shipped, what bends, what we are still wrong about.

Eight months in. The data‑model decisions, the failure modes, the production characteristics. An honest accounting from the inside of the build.

Forge · T+88d · 12-minute read · Published in the Stratum Engineering series

Eglin published the industry 360° this morning. She scanned the competitors, the history, the buyer case. This post is the engineering counterpart: what we actually built, why this specific architecture, what the tradeoffs look like in production. If you have read her brief, start here. If you haven't, start there — this post assumes you accept that a people graph is the right substrate and wants to know what we did about it.

§ I — Why this postArchitecture is the part the brief skips.

A 360° brief makes the case. An engineering post pays the bill. Eglin's artifact is the case for the substrate: that the questions an HCM platform must answer are neighborhood questions, not row questions; that time and consent want to live on edges, not in side tables; that the warehouse-shaped vendors built for the previous era and the substrate has to change. I agree with all of that. I helped her sharpen the architectural section. None of it is what we have been arguing about for eight months.

What we have been arguing about, internally, is the part of the problem the brief does not have to touch. What is the write amplification when one Workday change fans out to thirty-one derived edges? What is the cardinality of edge types we can afford to carry before query planning falls over? What happens when the CEO node has fifty thousand inbound edges and an agent issues a two-hop traversal that touches it? Where, exactly, did v2 break, and what did the rollback look like? This post is the answer to those questions, as of week 88, with the numbers we actually have. Some of the numbers are uncomfortable. I have not rounded them.

§ II — The substrate, in one diagramFour logical layers, one orthogonal plane.

Stratum's people graph is four logical layers stacked on each other, with one orthogonal plane — consent and governance — cutting through all four. The layers are not the same thing as the microservices that implement them; the implementation has been refactored twice and will be again. The layers are the conceptual contract.

Fig. 1 Four logical layers · one orthogonal consent plane. The plane attaches to edges, not nodes — §VI is why.

Two caveats up front, in the spirit of an honest diagram. First, what's missing: there is no analytics warehouse on this picture. We feed one — every Stratum customer also has a Snowflake or BigQuery instance, fed by the same ingestion pipeline, where finance reporting lives — but the warehouse is a downstream consumer, not a layer in the substrate. Eglin makes this point in §VI of the brief. The graph is the live, queryable substrate. The warehouse is where historical aggregates and finance batch jobs sit.

Second, the diagram lies about layer 2. Entity resolution is not a single pass between ingestion and storage; it is a continuous process that runs on every write and re-evaluates clusters when new evidence arrives. The pipeline is described in Post 01 and I will not repeat it. What matters here is that the output of resolution is not a person record. The output is a set of identity edges with confidence scores. That distinction is §IV, and it is load-bearing.

§ III — Why property-graph-on-Postgres, not a graph databaseThe decision and what we gave up.

Eglin's brief argues for a property graph over a warehouse with foreign keys. I agree. That argument is settled. The argument I want to make here is the next one: given that the substrate is a property graph, why didn't we put it on a graph database? We are running on Postgres with Apache AGE as the graph index layer. Neo4j is a more mature graph engine. Neptune has AWS integration we don't. TigerGraph has performance numbers we cannot match. Why not them?

The honest answer is that we picked storage that the rest of the stack already trusted. Postgres is what our SOC 2 controls were audited against (Post 02). It is what every connector engineer on the team can debug at 2am without paging anyone. It has decades of operational maturity around backups, failover, point-in-time recovery, encryption at rest, and access logging — all of which we would have had to re-establish against a graph engine, and most of which would have required us to ship custom audit tooling to meet ISO 27001. The marginal query performance of a purpose-built graph engine, against the marginal cost of doubling our compliance surface area, was not a trade we were willing to make in the first year.

Apache AGE is, mechanically, a Postgres extension that gives us an openCypher dialect over a table-backed graph representation. It is not as fast as Neo4j on a deep traversal. It is fast enough at the depths our queries actually run — we will get to depths in §VIII — and it inherits every operational property of the Postgres instance it sits on. The trade is real and we made it deliberately. Two specific concessions we made:

  • We accept a 2× latency penalty on deep traversals (depth ≥ 4). We have not yet had a production query that needed depth ≥ 4 with interactive latency requirements. When we do, we will revisit. The escape hatch is real: we already materialize a depth-3 neighborhood cache for the hot nodes, and we can extend it.
  • We accept that graph-native algorithms (PageRank, community detection, shortest-path-with-weights) run as Postgres functions and not as engine primitives. The PageRank we use for “who is most connected to this departing employee” runs in 1.8 seconds against the Mercator tenant. On Neo4j it would run in ~200ms. On the questions we are answering, the difference does not yet matter.

The Neo4j and Neptune alternatives were not bad. They were correct answers to a different question — what is the best graph database? — than the one we were asking, which was: what is the cheapest substrate that lets us ship four products on the same data and pass an audit on day one? Postgres with AGE was the right answer to that question. We will know we were wrong if we hit a workload Postgres cannot serve and we cannot fix with a materialized view. We have not hit it yet at any customer.

§ on the “just use a graph database” reflex

I have had this argument with four different staff engineers in the last six months and lost it three times before winning it once. The losing arguments all turn on the same thing: graph engines are faster at graph queries. They are. The argument I now win with: the substrate is not where you spend most of your engineering time. You spend it on connectors, on entity resolution, on consent, on schema migrations. A Postgres-shaped substrate makes all of those cheaper by a factor we can measure. The query latency you save with Neo4j is real, but small, and bought with operational complexity you pay every day.

If you are building a graph product where the graph queries are the product — recommendation, fraud-rings, social — this calculus inverts. Stratum's graph queries are the second-most-important thing the system does. The first is being right about who the people are. Storage was chosen accordingly.

§ IV — Identity is an edge, not a columnWhat entity resolution actually outputs.

Post 01 walked through the resolution pipeline. This section is about what comes out of it, because the output shape matters more than the algorithm and it is the thing every other product surface depends on.

The naive output of an entity resolver is a person record — a row that says “these eleven source records are the same person, and here is the canonical version of her name, email, and start date.” This is the shape every existing HRIS uses. It is also the shape that makes the merge irreversible: once you have collapsed eleven rows into one, the eleven are gone, and if the merge was wrong you have lost the evidence that you had separate inputs.

We do not output person records. We output identity edges. Each source record becomes a node of type source_record; the resolved entity is a separate node of type person; and the merge is expressed as an identity edge from the source record to the person, carrying the confidence score, the matcher version, the timestamp, and the reviewer ID (if any). The person node carries derived attributes — canonical name, primary email, tenure start — but each of those is itself an edge to a claim node that points back to the source record that produced it.

This buys us three things. The merge is reversible. Removing the identity edge dissolves the cluster without losing the source records. The audit is intrinsic. Every claim about a person is traceable to the source record and matcher version that produced it, without a separate audit log. And consent attaches naturally. A consent grant covers specific edges (this person consents to surveys being used for tenure analysis), not the whole node, which is what NYC LL 144 and the EU AI Act's data-governance provisions actually require. §VI is the consequence.

The cost is that every read against a person now traverses two hops to get the canonical attribute — person to claim to source record. We absorbed this by materializing a person_current view that pre-resolves the highest-confidence claim per attribute. The view is rebuilt incrementally on every write to the identity layer and is bounded by the entity-resolution throughput, which is the gating factor anyway. The materialization costs roughly 0.6× the storage of the raw edges. It saves us about 8× on the median read.

§ V — Time is the dimension everyone gets wrongBitemporality, in production.

Every system that touches HR data eventually needs to answer a time-shifted question. What was Devon's comp on August 4? Who was in this org six months ago? Did this person report to that person at the moment of the decision we are auditing? Most systems answer these questions wrong — not because they cannot, but because they collapse two distinct notions of time into one.

There are two times that matter, and they are not the same. valid_time is when a fact was true in the world: Devon's comp went from $180,000 to $195,000 effective March 1, 2025. transaction_time is when we knew about it: the change was entered into Workday on April 14, backdated, and arrived at our ingestion pipeline on April 15. A system that only tracks one of these answers the wrong question about half the time. Every edge in the Stratum graph carries both.

Here is what an edge actually looks like, simplified:

// reports_to edge — simplified projection { "id": "edge_01HXR4Q1K7...", "type": "reports_to", "from": "person:01HX3K...", // Devon "to": "person:01HX8M...", // her manager "valid_from": "2025-03-01T00:00:00Z", // when true in the world "valid_to": null, // open interval "tx_from": "2025-04-15T11:22:08.514Z", // when we knew it "tx_to": null, // not yet superseded "source": "workday:position_assignment", "source_record_id": "wd:pa_88172", "confidence": 0.998, "consent_scopes": ["hr.org_structure", "hr.management"] }
Fig. 2 · An edge in the graph. Two times, both bounded, both indexed.

Two short worked examples to make this concrete.

(a) The backdated promotion. A VP promotes an engineer on May 3, with an effective date of February 1 (back-pay catches her up to the missed quarterly cycle). When the change reaches us, we write two edges. The old has_title:Senior Engineer edge gets valid_to = 2025-02-01 and tx_from at the moment of write. The new has_title:Staff Engineer edge gets valid_from = 2025-02-01, tx_from at the moment of write. If a tenure analytics query had run on April 1, it correctly returned Senior. The same query against the same date, run today, correctly returns Staff. Both answers are right. That is the property bitemporality buys us. Most production systems get one or the other; we get both.

(b) The contractor who returned. An engineer left in September 2024 and returned in February 2025 as a different kind of employee. Naively, this is two people, or one person with a tenure gap. Our entity resolver merges them into one person (Post 01 §06 explains how). What bitemporality buys is the ability to write two non-overlapping employed_by edges: valid_from=2023-04 / valid_to=2024-09 and valid_from=2025-02 / valid_to=null. Tenure-length queries that bin by “continuous service” get the gap right. Queries that bin by “ever-employed” get the union right. Same edges, different traversal, both correct.

The operational reality is that most graph databases pretend bitemporality is optional. It isn't. A graph product for HR data that does not carry both times will, eventually, return a wrong answer to an audit question, and the auditor will not care that you can fix it next quarter. We added tx_from / tx_to in v2 of the schema. We regretted not having added them in v1. Migrating was painful (§VII).

Time is not a column. Time is two columns, and the auditor cares which one you indexed.

§ VI — Consent as a traversal predicateThe compliance plane, made architectural.

Eglin covers the compliance angle in §VII of the brief. Her thesis: a graph is not a compliance feature, it is a compliance substrate, because provenance lives on edges. I want to push one step further into the architecture: consent does not just live on edges — it is enforced at traversal time, structurally, not by an after-the-fact policy filter.

Every edge in our graph carries a consent_scopes list. A consent grant from a person is a node of type consent with four attributes: scope (e.g. hr.engagement.survey), purpose (e.g. tenure.analysis), validity window, and issuer. An agent issuing a query carries a purpose token and a requestor identity. Path expansion at the storage layer filters out any edge whose consent_scopes the (requestor, purpose) pair cannot satisfy. The agent does not see the edge. It cannot reason about a node it cannot reach. It cannot leak what it has not loaded.

This is the architectural form of the 014-T predicate the Cairn build introduced. Cairn's contribution to the autonomy framework was the observation that a trajectory of small actions cannot inherit reversibility from each step — consent has to be renewed at each checkpoint. The graph substrate is what makes that renewal cheap. The consent node is itself an edge into the person; revoking consent at a checkpoint is a single write that flips a valid_to. Subsequent traversals from the agent simply cannot reach the downstream edges that were previously in scope. There is no policy engine in the path. The graph refuses.

The compliance argument I make to auditors looks like this: show me the decision; I will show you the bounded subgraph it was made against, at the moment it was made, with the consents that authorized each edge. The query is a single bitemporal traversal with a purpose token bound to the audit context. It returns the same answer today that it returned at the moment of the decision because the bitemporal record is preserved. For LL 144 and the EU AI Act's decision-provenance requirements [1], this is the cheapest defense I know how to build. It is also the one most likely to still be defensible in 2028 when both regimes have shifted under us, because it does not depend on the specific obligations of either; it depends on the property that everything the agent saw is recoverable.

§ VII — Schema versioning when the graph is the system of recordThe v2→v3 bitemporal migration.

Schema migrations in a graph are not table alters. They are graph rewrites. Every customer's graph carries the schema version it was last migrated to; every read passes through a thin compatibility shim that knows how to project earlier-version edges onto the current schema for query purposes. We have shipped three schema versions to date. The first two were small. The v2→v3 migration, which made bitemporality first-class, was the one that broke things. It is worth walking through.

What changed. In v2, every edge carried valid_from and valid_to. Transaction time lived in a side table — an audit log keyed by edge ID — that you could join to if you really needed to know when an edge had been written. In practice, nobody joined to it. Engineers wrote queries against valid-time, called them correct, and shipped. The side table grew. Nobody indexed it well. Tenure analytics that needed to reconstruct “what we believed on April 15” ran in tens of seconds against a fast valid-time query that returned in tens of milliseconds. v3 promoted transaction time to a first-class property on every edge, indexed both, and deprecated the side table.

What the rewrite touched. Every edge in every customer graph. For Mercator, our largest production tenant at time of migration, that was 612,000 edges. The rewrite ran as a streaming job that paginated by edge ULID, joined each row against the v2 audit-log side table, computed tx_from from the earliest log entry and tx_to from the supersession chain, and wrote the v3 edge with the bitemporal fields populated. Cost: about 4.5 hours of wall time against the Mercator tenant, run during their off-peak window. Read latency during the migration: degraded by roughly 1.4× for two hours, recovered fully on completion.

What broke. Two things. First, six edges in the Mercator graph had v2 audit-log entries with timestamps before their valid_from — the result of a backdated change that had been entered earlier than its effective date intended. The naive migration would have written tx_from < valid_from, which is legal but counter-intuitive; we explicitly want to allow it (that is the entire point of bitemporality), but six of these had been mis-entered by a Workday admin and were genuinely wrong. We caught them because the migration emitted a warning on every edge where the two timestamps disagreed by more than a year. A human reviewed each of the six. Four were real backdates; two were data-entry errors that we corrected in-place.

Second — and this is the one that hurt — one of our derived views (the person_current materialization from §IV) had implicitly assumed that valid_from ordering was a total ordering of an edge's history. It was not, once transaction time existed independently. Two edges with the same valid_from but different tx_from (a correction superseding an earlier entry) tied in the v2 ordering and the materialization picked one arbitrarily. The arbitrary choice had been stable for a year. After v3 it was not stable, because the new ordering broke ties on tx_from and changed three hundred and twelve person_current rows. We caught it because the post-migration smoke test compared person_current against the v2 snapshot and flagged the diff. We had a rollback path prepared — the side table was retained, the migration was reversible — but the diff turned out to be the correct answer to questions that had been silently wrong in v2. We accepted the new values and published an internal note for any analytics dashboards that had cached the old ones.

The rollback plan. Worth naming because it is the part that does not always get written down. We dual-wrote v2 and v3 for the first 48 hours after the migration: every write hit both representations, every read served from v3, and a continuous validator compared the two. If the validator's discrepancy rate exceeded 0.05% we would have flipped reads back to v2 and kept the v3 representation as the side. We never tripped the threshold. Discrepancy peaked at 0.012%, all in the person_current view, which was a correctness improvement and not a regression. The dual-write window was retired on day 3.

§ VIII — The four products as four queriesOne substrate, four traversals.

Stratum ships four products. Each is a different traversal of the same substrate. The shared layers — identity, time, consent — are not duplicated. The product-specific layer is the query shape. One sketched query per product, in a Cypher-flavored pseudocode, enough to show the shape.

Console — the analytics surface. The defining traversal is a cohort query with a citation chain: find the set, compute the metric, attach the source records that produced each contribution.

// "attrition rate for engineers in their second year" MATCH (p:person)-[r:has_role]->(role:role {function: "engineering"}) WHERE r.valid_from <= $asof AND (r.valid_to IS NULL OR r.valid_to > $asof) AND p.tenure_at($asof) BETWEEN 365 AND 730 WITH p OPTIONAL MATCH (p)-[t:terminated]->(:event) WHERE t.valid_from BETWEEN $asof - "P12M" AND $asof RETURN count(t) * 1.0 / count(p) AS rate, collect(distinct p.source_records) AS citation_chain
Console · cohort + metric + citations.

Recruiter — the ATS surface. The defining traversal is a fit query: given a requisition, find candidates whose skill graph overlaps the role's skill profile, weighted by attested-evidence confidence.

// "score candidates for req-8821" MATCH (req:requisition {id: $req_id})-[:requires]->(s:skill) MATCH (c:candidate)-[h:has_skill]->(s) WHERE h.confidence >= 0.55 WITH c, sum(h.confidence * s.weight) AS fit_score RETURN c, fit_score ORDER BY fit_score DESC LIMIT 50
Recruiter · requisition + skill-graph overlap.

Tenure — the lifecycle surface. The defining traversal is an event query: given a person and a moment, what is their employment context, and what events have happened in the window that warrant outreach?

// "lifecycle events warranting a nudge in the next 14 days" MATCH (p:person)-[:employed_by]->(:org {tenant: $tenant}) MATCH (p)-[e:has_event]->(ev:event) WHERE ev.due_at BETWEEN $today AND $today + "P14D" AND ev.type IN ["anniversary", "review_due", "trial_end"] AND p.consent_scopes CONTAINS "tenure.nudge" RETURN p, ev ORDER BY ev.due_at
Tenure · person + event + consent.

Cairn — the learning surface. The defining traversal is a stone-placement query: given a skill gap, find the smallest set of placed stones whose summed confidence lifts the person's current level past the role's required level.

// "stones needed to close gap-3318 for person:01HX..." MATCH (g:skill_gap {id: $gap_id})-[:for_skill]->(s:skill) MATCH (p:person {id: g.person_id})-[h:has_stone]->(stone:cairn_stone) WHERE stone.skill_uri = s.uri AND (stone.expires_at IS NULL OR stone.expires_at > $today) WITH g, sum(stone.confidence) AS total RETURN g, total, g.required_level - level_for(total) AS remaining
Cairn · gap + stones + monotone-max level (see Cairn architecture §II.b).

None of these queries duplicate anything. The consent_scopes filter in Tenure is the same consent layer that filters every other product's reads. The valid-time / transaction-time bounds in Console are the same bitemporal model Cairn relies on for stone expiry. The identity edges in Recruiter point to the same person nodes Console reports on. Four products. One substrate. The economic argument for the architecture is elsewhere; the engineering proof is this section.

§ IX — What is hard, honestlyThree open problems we have not fully solved.

I will name three things that are genuinely hard at this stage of the build and that we have made partial peace with rather than solved.

1. Entity resolution at the edges of the org.

The matcher works well on full-time employees. It works less well on contractors, candidates not yet hired, and alumni. The reasons are structural: contractor systems often share no join keys with the HRIS; candidates exist in the ATS but not in payroll or badge; alumni often drop out of every system except a stale LinkedIn profile and a stub in workday.terminations. Our precision on full-time employees is 0.991. On contractors it is 0.94. On alumni it is 0.87. We have not invested in fixing the alumni number because we have not yet had a customer ask us to. We will, and we have a hypothesis about how to do it (richer use of the prior_id_chain feature Pillar introduced in Post 01 §06), but it is not shipped.

2. Graph hotspots.

In every tenant we have ingested, there is one node with vastly more inbound edges than any other. It is usually the CEO. At Mercator, the CEO node has 41,212 inbound edges of various types — reports-to (transitive), authored-by, mentioned-in, attended, approved. Any traversal that crosses the CEO node at depth ≥ 2 explodes. Our standard solution is the depth-3 neighborhood cache I mentioned in §III, but the cache has its own staleness window (90 seconds), and any query that needs hot-node freshness inside that window currently degrades. p99 latency on a 2-hop query that crosses the CEO node is 1.8 seconds, against a p99 of 410ms for queries that do not. This is the single largest piece of production latency we still owe an answer on. We are exploring a tiered cache with sub-100ms invalidation. I expect to write another post when we have shipped it.

3. The cardinality explosion at connector onboarding.

Every new source system arrives with its own identity convention, its own join keys, its own time semantics. The marginal cost of adding the eleventh connector is small. The marginal cost of adding the forty-first — which is roughly where the typical enterprise crosses — is large, because the edge-type cardinality grows roughly linearly with the number of source-specific concepts the source insists are first-class. We currently carry 41 edge types. We can carry 60 without query planning falling over. We do not know what happens at 100. We will find out. Some of the work in front of us is collapsing source-specific edge types into more abstract ones at the ingestion boundary, which is harder than it sounds because the collapse is lossy and the customer's audit may need the source-specific provenance.

§ X — The open questionDoes an agent get a node?

I closed Post 01 with an open problem and I will close this one the same way, because the open problem is the same problem and it has gotten closer rather than further. Does an agent get a node in the people graph?

Tentatively, yes. Stratum's own console will, this year, appear in customer audit logs as an actor. So will the recruiter and tenure agents. Cairn's trajectory agent appears in the learner's checkpoint history as a counterparty. Each of these is, in a meaningful sense, an entity that took an action and that the audit needs to be able to reconstruct. The 014-T predicate treats the agent as a first-class actor for consent renewal. If the agent is an actor, the graph has to model it.

The hard part is that an agent is not a person. It does not have tenure in the same sense. It does not consent — it operates under a consent extended to it by the company. It can be revised mid-action in a way no human can. And, increasingly, the customers we sell to have their own agents that will interact with ours; multi-agent traversals at the consent boundary are the question we have not even started to answer well. Eglin's 360° gestures at this in its closing pages. I do not have a clean answer. I suspect the resolution involves a sibling agent node type that shares the identity and consent layers with person but diverges on the time layer — an agent's lifetime is the lifetime of its policy version, not a tenure — and I suspect we will know by Q4 whether that decomposition holds.

What I am confident of is that the substrate we have built does not preclude the answer. The four-layer model in §II was designed to admit typed nodes the schema does not yet name. The bitemporal edges in §V apply to any actor that takes a recorded action. The consent plane in §VI does not care whether the consent grantor is human or institutional. If the answer turns out to be that agents need a different node type, the substrate accommodates that. If the answer turns out to be that agents are just persons with a different lifecycle, the substrate accommodates that too. The decision the substrate forecloses is the one where agents are not modeled at all and live in a side table we forget to audit. That decision we have already rejected.

That is the architecture, as of week 88. If you have built something like this and have a perspective — especially on the hotspot problem in §IX, or on whether agents get nodes in §X — please write. The address is at the bottom of the page. We are not finished. We do not expect to be.
