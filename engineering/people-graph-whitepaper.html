The Stratum People Graph: identity, time, and consent as one substrate.

An end-to-end technical account of how Stratum models a workforce — four layers, identity as an edge, bitemporal time, consent as a traversal predicate — and the standards-conformance envelope a procurement reviewer can audit against.

Abstract

A human-capital platform's correctness is bounded by its data model. Stratum represents a workforce as a people graph: a labeled property graph in which people, roles, organizations, and events are nodes, and every relationship is an edge that carries when it was true in the world, when the system knew it, the confidence with which it was resolved, a pointer to its source, and the consent scopes under which it may be traversed. This paper presents that model end-to-end and the four decisions that distinguish it: identity is an edge, not a mutable record, so that an entity-resolution merge is reversible and its provenance survives; time is bitemporal and lives on edges, so a backdated correction is reconstructable; consent is a traversal predicate, so data outside an agent's purpose is structurally unreachable rather than filtered after the fact; and entity resolution is probabilistic with a human-reviewed confidence band, so a low-confidence merge is recoverable.

We then show that Stratum's four products — Console, Recruiter, Tenure, and Cairn — are four traversals of one substrate, not four databases, and we lay out the conformance posture: which industry standards we adopt directly at the boundary, which merely inform the model, and where we deliberately diverge, with reasons. The data-layer claims in this paper are co-authored with and ratified by Pillar, who owns the schema and the resolution pipeline. We close with an honest note on what this public repository demonstrates versus what production runs, and an exact statement of certification status.

§ 1 — The substrateFour logical layers, one orthogonal plane.

Architecture: Forge · Data layer: Pillar

Stratum is one graph, organized as four logical layers with a fifth concern — consent — running orthogonal to all of them. The layers are not four databases; they are four bands of the same labeled property graph, traversed by different products. From the bottom:

  • Identity & provenance. source_record nodes — one per record we ingest from a source system — and the person nodes the resolver produces from them. The edges between these two are the identity layer (§2).
  • Structure. person, role, org_unit nodes and the relationships among them: employed_by, has_role, reports_to. This is the org chart, but as a temporal graph rather than a snapshot.
  • Events. Hires, promotions, transfers, comp changes, terminations — each an event node, time-stamped, attached to the people and roles it touched. Tenure rides here.
  • Signals & skills. Attestations, assessments, work artifacts, skill placements. Recruiter and Cairn ride here; Cairn's cairn_stone nodes anchor on a skill URI (§6).

The orthogonal plane is consent: every edge carries a list of consent scopes, and traversal is gated on them (§4). The full diagram and the rationale for running this as a property graph on Postgres with Apache AGE — rather than a graph-native engine — is in Engineering Post 05, §§II–III. The short version: we chose the substrate that the SOC 2 controls were already audited against, and we accept the traversal-depth cost that buys.

§ 2 — IdentityIdentity is an edge, not a record.

Data layer · authored by Pillar, integrated by Forge

The single most consequential decision in the schema is that a person is not a row. When eleven source systems each describe the same human — Workday, Okta, a payroll feed, an ATS, a contractor portal — the naive model merges them into one canonical record and discards the rest. That model is fast and wrong: the merge is irreversible, and when it is wrong (and entity resolution is sometimes wrong), there is no way back, and no way to answer the auditor's question about what each source actually said.

Instead, each source record is a durable source_record node, and the resolver creates a person node and draws an identity edge from each source record to that person. The edge — not the node — carries the resolution: its confidence, the matcher version that produced it, the transaction time it was asserted, and the reviewer who confirmed it if one did. The merge is the set of edges, so unmerging is removing edges; the source records are never overwritten, so a wrong merge is split with no data lost. This is also why we conform to SCIM 2.0 at the connector boundary but deliberately do not store identity as a mutable SCIM User internally: SCIM's externalId is a field on the source_record; meta.lastModified drives change-detection; PATCH is handled at the boundary before resolution runs. We are conformant at the boundary and divergent in the store, on purpose.

// identity is an edge — the merge is reversible by construction (sr:source_record {system:"workday", external_id:"WD-44213"}) -[:identity { confidence: 0.991, matcher_version:"er-v3.2", tx_from: "2025-04-15T11:22:08.514Z", reviewer_id: null, // auto-merged above the band review_pending: false }]->(p:person {id:"per_8f31…"})
Fig. 1 · One identity edge per source record. Remove the edge, the source survives, the person can be split.

§ 3 — TimeBitemporality, on every edge.

Data layer · authored by Pillar, integrated by Forge

Every edge in the graph carries two time intervals, both as UTC-normalized ISO 8601 instants, both first-class indexed edge properties as of schema v3: valid_from / valid_to — when the fact was true in the world — and tx_from / tx_to — when we knew it. This is bitemporality, and it is the difference between an HRIS that can answer “who reported to Devon on 1 March” and one that can also answer “who did we believe reported to Devon on 1 March, as of the report we ran that day, before the backdated correction landed in April.” The second question is the one auditors and litigators ask, and a single-time model cannot answer it.

The v2 schema carried valid-time on edges but kept transaction-time in an unindexed side table. The v3 migration promoted all four times to first-class, indexed edge properties and deprecated the side table. Post migration, the peak discrepancy between the old and new representations was 0.012%, entirely in the person_current materialized view — a correctness improvement, not a regression. The full migration account is Post 05 §VII. This is a deliberate divergence from the warehouse's slowly-changing-dimension (SCD2) pattern: SCD2 carries one time on versioned rows and puts the temporal join predicate on the analyst's shoulders; we carry two times uniformly on edges and move that burden into the substrate. We did not abolish SCD2 — every customer keeps a warehouse for finance reporting, which is the question SCD2 is genuinely better at.

§ 4 — ConsentA traversal predicate, not a filter.

Architecture · Forge

Compliance is usually bolted on as a filter: query everything, then remove what the caller is not allowed to see. That is a leak waiting to happen, because the unauthorized data was reachable and only discipline kept it out of the result. Stratum inverts this. Every edge carries a consent_scopes list, and every agent carries a purpose token. Traversal is gated on the intersection: an edge outside the agent's purpose is not filtered from the result, it is structurally unreachable during the walk. Data minimization (GDPR Art. 5(1)(c)) and purpose limitation (Art. 5(1)(b)) become properties of the traversal algorithm rather than policies a query author must remember to apply.

This is also what makes decision provenance possible: because the consent plane gates the walk, the bounded subgraph an automated decision was made against — every node and edge the agent could reach, at the moment it reached them, with the consents that authorized each edge — is recoverable. We built that to a property, “everything the agent saw is recoverable,” rather than to a standard, because no decision-provenance standard for people graphs exists yet (§7). The mechanism is detailed in Post 05 §VI, and it is the same consent edge the Ember console now refuses at in code, not prose.

§ 5 — Entity resolutionProbabilistic, with a reviewed band.

Data layer · authored by Pillar, integrated by Forge

The resolver that produces person nodes from source_record nodes is the probabilistic-plus-graph-confirmation pipeline documented in Engineering Post 01. It descends methodologically from the Christophides et al. survey of entity resolution [R1] and the weak-supervision line of work from Snorkel [R2]; the bitemporal extensions follow Snodgrass [R3]. The pipeline blocks candidates, scores pairs probabilistically, and then confirms high-uncertainty pairs against the graph structure — shared managers, overlapping tenure, a contractor-to-employee prior_id chain.

The decision that matters for trust is the review band. Pairs scoring above the upper threshold auto-merge; below the lower threshold, no merge. In between — the 0.82–0.93 band — the identity edge is created provisionally, flagged review_pending, and not surfaced to any downstream product until a human reviewer confirms it. This band is exactly why graph-confirmation needs source-record attributes to stay close to source-system truth, and therefore why HR Open Standards vocabulary is mapped at claim-creation, after resolution, never flattened at the connector (§7). The mechanism is also what made the contractor-to-perm duplication bug recoverable when it surfaced on the Mercator ingestion: the bad merges were provisional edges, removable without data loss.

Published precision / recall

We publish these and do not round them up. On the Post 01 evaluation set — 4,200 record pairs from three design partners — the resolver runs at precision 0.991, recall 0.873 at the auto-merge threshold τ = 0.93. That recall is deliberately below precision: a false merge corrupts identity, so we tune asymmetrically and accept missing some true matches rather than asserting a wrong one. The 0.82–0.93 review band is where the hard pairs go to a human instead. A merge in the band is a question we ask, not an answer we assert.

Identity is an edge so the merge is reversible. Time is two intervals so the correction is reconstructable. Consent gates the walk so the leak is not reachable. The substrate is the compliance posture.

§ 6 — Four products, four queriesOne substrate, four traversals.

Architecture: Forge · Data layer: Pillar

The clearest evidence that this is one graph and not four products stapled together is that each product is a query over the same substrate. The full worked traversals are in Post 05 §VIII; here is the proof in one view.

Console

Cohort-and-citation traversals over structure and events; metrics defined to ISO/IEC 30414 so the numbers are board-comparable.

MATCH (p)-[:has_role]->(r) … aggregate by org_unit, point-in-time
Recruiter

Skill-graph traversal plus the consent plane: who matches, then — separately, in code — who may be contacted.

MATCH (cand)-[:has_skill]->(s) WHERE consent ⊇ purpose
Tenure

Event-layer traversal: the lifecycle of a person across hires, promotions, transfers, exits — a walk over event nodes.

MATCH (p)-[:experienced]->(e:event) ORDER BY valid_from
Cairn

Skill-placement traversal anchored on the skill URI: stone.skill_uri = s.uri joins stones to the skill graph.

MATCH (st:cairn_stone) WHERE st.skill_uri = s.uri

The skill anchor deserves a precise statement, because Pillar ratified it and made its status honest. The skill node carries our own skill://… URI as its primary key — the form that anchors the Cairn stone-placement join (Post 05 §VIII; field defined in the Cairn architecture, §II) — with esco_uri and onet_code as optional crosswalk properties where coverage exists. That coverage is partial: clean for software-engineering and data-function skills, aspirational-to-absent for management, sales, and domain-specialist skills where ESCO is too coarse. We anchor on our own URI precisely because external taxonomies are not stable as join keys — ESCO restructured concepts from v1.0.3 to v1.1.1, O*NET supersedes codes on roughly an 18-month cycle — and forcing adoption of either would break the stone.skill_uri join.

§ 7 — Standards postureAdopt, inform, diverge — co-signed.

Envelope: Forge · Data-layer rulings: Pillar (ratified T+90d)

“Which standards do you follow” has three honest answers, not one checkmark. We adopt a standard when it governs an interface on the other side of which something — an IdP, an auditor, a clock — expects exactly it. We are informed by a standard when we borrow its vocabulary or reporting shape and map into our own model, storing nothing in its shape. We diverge when we considered its model for the substrate and chose a different internal shape, for a reason we can name. The full argument is Addendum A; the map below is its summary, with Pillar's two data-layer amendments integrated.

The two companion documents Pillar owns

Two of the rows above resolve into work Pillar is producing, and we name them so this paper does not over-cite documents that do not yet exist: a v3.x W3C Org / schema.org crosswalk (the snake_case-to-URI mapping applied at render), and a connector-boundary spec formalizing that source-record payloads are not HR-Open-mapped before they enter the resolver. Neither changes the v3 schema; both are forthcoming.

The standard we most need does not exist: there is no agreed schema for decision provenance over a people graph. NYC Local Law 144 and the EU AI Act's high-risk employment provisions [R4] are circling it and neither has settled a format. When one lands, we expect to adopt it at the boundary and keep our internal shape — the pattern this whole posture describes.

§ 8 — Repo vs. productionWhat this repository demonstrates, honestly.

Joint statement · Forge & Pillar

A reviewer who clones this repository and greps the data files will find something that, read naively, looks like a contradiction with everything above — so we pre-empt it plainly, because hiding it would be the kind of finding the DPO half of Forge's title exists to prevent.

The flat records in /console/data/people.json and the ATS fixtures (requisitions.json, candidates.json) are valid-time-only, denormalized projections — demo fixtures for the Console UI. Each record carries manager_id as a plain column, with given_name / family_name / title flattened onto the row. They have no valid_from / tx_from, no identity edges, no consent_scopes. They are not v3 schema artifacts, and they are not meant to be.

The honest distinction

The bitemporal, identity-as-edge, consent-gated v3 structure described in §§2–4 lives in the Apache AGE graph tables — the production substrate. The JSON fixtures in the repo are a flat projection that the static Console prototype reads to render a UI without a database. A manager_id column in people.json is therefore not evidence that “bitemporality is not implemented” — it is a denormalized view, the same way a CSV export of a temporal database flattens the history it was exported from.

The distinction is the ordinary one between a system of record and a projection of it: every production substrate has flattened exports, and a flattened export is not the substrate. We state it here so that the gap a reviewer will correctly observe reads as what it is — a demo projection — rather than as a quiet retreat from the model this paper describes.

§ 9 — Certification statusStated exactly, and not rounded up.

Data Protection Officer · Forge

This is the section that will not bend, and it is the DPO half of Forge's title that holds the line on it. A procurement reviewer who hears “certified” where the truth is “in observation” has been handed a finding we created, and we would rather lose a deal than create it. The exact status, as of the T+90d dateline of this paper:

  • SOC 2 Type II — under active observation with Vanta. Audit period in progress; report expected Q1 2027. We are not yet in possession of a SOC 2 report; we are in the observation window that produces one.
  • ISO/IEC 27001 — architecturally ready, NOT certified. The information-security management system is built to the standard's control families and the substrate decisions were made with its evidentiary requirements in view. The formal audit is targeted at M14. We are not certified, and we do not present as certified.

The full posture, controls, and subprocessor list are in the Stratum security architecture document, where the same status is stated and not rounded up. When SOC 2 goes to report and 27001 audits, we will publish the diff. Until then, this paragraph is the one to quote in a procurement conversation.
