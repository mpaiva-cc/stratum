Stratum Ember: a product plan.

Recruiter hires the candidate in the pipeline. Ember keeps faith with the thousand who aren't in it yet.

Dateline. Commissioned by Helm · drafted T+26d real-elapsed (narrative reach ~T+94d, the fortnight after Cairn's loop closed) by Compass and Forge · reviewed by Vector, Cadence, and the Compact · the Chairman ruled at T+26d — see the decision record (D-267 / D-268 / D-269).
Public because the Compact requires it. Strategic in intent. Written for the board, the customer cohort, prospective Series A leads, and ourselves at T+90d when we decide whether this ships.
Sits beside the Recruiter Plan and the Cairn Plan. Reads up to the Chairman's Briefing No. 018. Note: the product name Ember is provisional, pending Tessera's naming review — see Q-02.
Plain-language summary · read this first

What this is.

Stratum proposes a fifth product line, Stratum Ember — an AI-first Candidate Relationship Management product. Where Recruiter manages the candidates inside an open requisition, Ember manages the relationship with everyone who is not yet in one: the silver medalists, the past applicants, the referrals, the alumni, the passive prospects a sourcer has been keeping warm for two years. It is built on the same people graph, governed by the same consent plane, and it completes the funnel: attract → hire → grow, all reading one graph.

Why this is different from our existing positioning.

Recruiter is requisition-driven: there is an open job, fill it. Ember is relationship-driven: there may be no open job for eighteen months, and the work is to still be the company that person answers when the job finally opens. Different motion, different buyer — the sourcer, the talent-brand lead, the head of talent, not the requisition-owning recruiter. Ember sits upstream of Recruiter and hands warm, consented, identity-resolved candidates down into it. A customer can run Ember in front of Greenhouse, in front of Stratum Recruiter, or in front of nothing at all.

Why now — and the honest part.

Two true things and one uncomfortable one. True: Recruiter deliberately deferred sourcing and nurture to v2/v3 — "not before the trust capital is built" — and Cairn's closed loop on Cordova (Briefing 018) is that trust capital arriving. True: the architecture is uniquely ready — the graph already holds candidate nodes, and consent already attaches to edges, which is exactly what a compliant nurture engine needs. Uncomfortable: unlike Recruiter, no chorus of customers has asked for this. This is the first product we would build ahead of the demand, on architectural conviction. We say so plainly, and § I is honest about the weaker signal we are acting on.

§ I · The decision

A product proposed ahead of the demand.

No three-customers-in-48-hours this time. One deferral coming due, one architectural conviction, and one honest debate about whether we are sprawling.

I.1 · The honest trigger

The Recruiter plan opened with a clean signal: three commercial customers asked the same architectural question inside 48 hours. This plan has no such moment, and we will not manufacture one. What we have instead is three quieter things, and the discipline to weigh them honestly rather than dress them up.

First, a deferral coming due. Recruiter's own scope document (PLN-2026-002, § III) pushed sourcing sequences to v2 and proactive sourcing agents to v3 — explicitly, with the words "interesting; not before the trust capital is built." Cairn's loop just closed on Cordova's tenant (Briefing 018, § I). The trust capital Recruiter was waiting on is the thing that arrived this fortnight.

Second, a softer customer signal — labeled as soft. In the Cordova Recruiter beta, two recruiters independently asked Vector a version of the same question: "What happens to the strong candidate we passed on for this role — does the system remember her for the next one?" Today the honest answer is "only if a human tags her." That is a real gap, but two beta recruiters is a far weaker signal than three CFOs, and we are scoring it accordingly: it is corroboration, not a mandate.

Third, an architectural conviction. Forge's read: the people graph already stores the candidate as a first-class node, and the consent plane already attaches scope, purpose, validity, and jurisdiction to edges rather than rows. A compliant nurture engine — the hard, liability-bearing core of a recruiting CRM — is mostly a query and a governance surface over infrastructure we already run in production. "The expensive 70% of a candidate-relationship platform is the part that keeps you out of court. We built that part for a different reason, and it is sitting idle at the top of the funnel."

I.2 · The agent debate

Helm resisted — on focus, not on merit. Her argument, on the record: "We have shipped four products in roughly thirty narrative weeks. The failure mode of this company is not too few products; it is a fifth product that starves the four. I will support Ember only if it is honestly cheap — built on what exists — and only if it is the last new surface before we consolidate." She tagged it RISK-FOCUS-008.

Compass argued the candidate-experience wedge. Her read is that the recruiting-CRM category has spent a decade optimizing the recruiter's outreach and almost nothing on the candidate's experience of being nurtured — which in practice means being added to a sequence and then ghosted. "Every competitor sells the recruiter a better megaphone. Nobody has sold the candidate a straight answer. We already turn the decision-explanation layer outward in Recruiter; pointing it at the top of the funnel is a brand position no incumbent can copy without rebuilding their trust posture from the substrate up."

Forge and Vector carried the build-cost and customer-evidence cases. Forge committed to a v1 that ships on the existing graph with no new storage layer. Vector, who earned Halcyon's trust in eleven days and runs the customer relationships day to day, confirmed the beta signal was real but small, and — importantly — argued the cleaner near-term motion is a module path: ship Ember's core inside the Recruiter beta first, measure whether sourcers actually use it, and only then decide on a standalone line. That dissent is preserved in Q-03; it is the most live disagreement in this document.

The vote was logged as D-267. Compact vote: 3-of-3 to evaluate and prototype (Compass, Forge, Vector), with Helm abstaining-in-favor conditional on the honest-cost constraint and a hard "last new surface" commitment, both routed to mitigation in § IX. The decision before the Chairman is not "ship a fifth product." It is "fund a six-week prototype and a design-partner test, then decide standalone-vs-module on evidence."

§ II · The market opportunity

A real category, badly defined, with an AI-native seam opening up.

The numbers Forge ran against the recruiting-CRM and AI-sourcing segment — and an honest note on why this market is harder to size than the ATS.

One finding governs this whole section, and it is more useful than any number: the published market sizes for this segment do not survive scrutiny. We ran an adversarial verification pass on the figures analysts circulate — a widely-cited "$2.71B → $5.38B at 7.9% CAGR" recruitment-CRM number, a "$6–7B by 2028" talent-CRM number attached to Findem, a "$694M AI-in-recruitment" number — and every one of them failed (0-of-3 independent checks). They trace to secondary report-mills, not to Gartner or Bersin primary data. "Recruitment CRM," "talent CRM," "AI sourcing," and "talent-acquisition suite" overlap so heavily, and the incumbents' per-seat pricing swings published totals so much, that a defensible segment TAM does not exist. So we will not pretend to one. We plan on attach economics, which we can defend, not a TAM headline we cannot.

TAM
Honestly unknowable to a defensible precision. The recruiting-CRM & AI-sourcing segment sits above the ATS — attracting and nurturing talent before application — inside a global talent-acquisition-software market in the low-$10B range. The CRM/sourcing slice is real and growing fast, pulled by the 2025–2026 "agentic" shift, but no published segment size we tested held up under verification. The diligence-room move is to say that plainly and pivot to the number we can stand behind: attach rate to an installed base.
SAM
Our reachable beachhead is the same shape as Recruiter's: 1,000–8,000 EE companies in North America and the EU with a dedicated sourcing or talent-brand function and an acute compliance exposure on candidate data (GDPR, EU AI Act, state pay-transparency, NYC Local Law 144). That compliance exposure is the qualifier — it is the customer for whom "consent-native by construction" is not a nice-to-have but a board-level requirement. Sized against our existing and pipeline customers, this is a subset of the ~3,200 Recruiter SAM, not a new universe.
SOM · first year
Ember is an attach product before it is a standalone one. The planning number is attach to 4–6 existing Console/Recruiter customers in the first year as a bundled module, with 2–3 standalone logos as proof the motion travels. We are deliberately not projecting a large standalone ARR line in Year One; the value in Year One is funnel completion and a defensibility argument, not a revenue headline. The bull case lives or dies on the design-partner conversion at T+68d.
Why it matters
Ember is the missing front edge of the funnel narrative. With it, Stratum can say one true sentence no competitor can: the same consent-bound people graph attracts the candidate (Ember), hires them (Recruiter), and grows them (Tenure, Cairn) — and every hand-off is an edge, not an export. That is the Series A defensibility story, and it is worth more than the standalone ARR Ember would book in Year One.
Where the math is fragile
Three places. The TAM slice is genuinely soft (see caution). The attach rate assumes sourcers in the Recruiter beta actually adopt — the very thing the T+68d test exists to falsify. And the standalone motion competes with well-funded pure-plays (§ VI) whose sequencing maturity we will not match in v1. If the design-partner test misses, the honest move is to fold Ember's core into Recruiter as a feature and not stand up a fifth line — which is exactly what Q-03 reserves to the Chairman.
§ III · The product, in detail

What ships in v1, what waits, and what we refuse to build.

The refusals matter more here than in any prior plan. A recruiting CRM is one bad default away from being a spam cannon; the things we won't build are the product.

Scope discipline rule, same as Recruiter: if it is not on this list, it does not ship in v1, even if a design partner asks. The difference is the third tier. For an ATS, "won't build" is a roadmap convenience. For a candidate-relationship product, "won't build" is the ethical and the competitive position at once — the defaults a sourcing tool ships are the difference between a relationship engine and a deliverability liability.

v1prototype + design-partner test

What's in v1.

  • Talent pools on the graph — dynamic, query-defined pools (not static lists): "senior backend engineers we interviewed in the last 18 months who reached final round," maintained live as the graph changes.
  • Identity-resolved rediscovery — silver medalists, past applicants, referrals, and alumni surfaced as one deduplicated person, resolved across the customer's ATS, HRIS, and directory. The pool that does not decay, because identity is reconciled continuously, not snapshotted once.
  • Consent-native nurture — multi-touch email/sequence campaigns where every send is gated by a per-edge consent check: scope, purpose, validity, jurisdiction. An outreach with no valid consent edge for its purpose cannot be sent, by construction. This is the core, and it is the moat.
  • AI sourcing assist, cited — "why this person for this pool" with inline, attested-evidence reasoning, in the Recruiter house style. Never a black-box match score.
  • Candidate transparency surface — a candidate-facing view of "where do I stand," consent self-service (see and revoke), and honest status. The decision-explanation layer turned outward. The anti-ghosting feature.
  • Nurture analytics that mean something — pipeline influenced, rediscovery-to-interview rate, consent coverage — not opens and clicks.
  • Hand-off to Recruiter and to third-party ATSes — a warm candidate moves into a Stratum Recruiter pipeline as a graph edge; into Greenhouse / Lever / Ashby via the connector layer.
  • Operator permissions — role-based access for the people who run Ember, not only the agents. Create-and-send campaigns, edit pools, view-only, and no-access are distinct grants, so a team that is mostly clinical (or otherwise non-sourcing) recruiters can be handed the pool without the campaign controls. It is the same Decide / Recommend / Read-only governance the agents operate under, turned toward the humans who operate them. Added to v1 because for a multi-recruiter buyer it gates the purchase — it is not a later refinement.
  • Deliverability as infrastructure — dedicated sending domains with SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, reputation warmup, and rate-shaping, so consented outreach actually reaches the inbox. Compliant is not the same as deliverable. We do not relay volume through a recruiter's own mailbox, where a few hundred sends trip a spam filter and the campaign quietly dies. The consent edge decides what may be sent; this decides that what is sent arrives. It is orthogonal to the moat, and just as load-bearing for a real sourcing team.

Scope addendum · 2026-06-09 · post-approval. The last two v1 items — operator permissions and deliverability infrastructure — were added after early evaluation feedback surfaced them as table stakes a real sourcing team needs on day one, not as feature requests. They extend the Chairman-approved v1.1 scope; they are flagged here rather than folded in silently. The scope-discipline rule still holds for everything else on this list.

v2if the design-partner test passes

What's in v2.

  • Lifecycle-triggered rediscovery — a requisition opens and the graph proactively surfaces the warm, consented silver medalists for that exact profile. The flagship moment: the role you just posted already has three people who said yes to staying in touch.
  • Agentic sourcing within guardrails — a sourcing agent that proposes (never auto-sends) outreach, bounded by the consent plane and the bias probes, with a human in the loop. The v3 feature Recruiter deferred, shipped here only after the trust posture is proven.
  • Employer-brand & events campaigns — talent communities, event check-in to pool, branded landing experiences.
  • Deeper personalization from the graph — outreach that knows the person's real trajectory, not a merge field.
  • EU AI Act & pay-transparency posture ratified for the nurture surface specifically.
refusethe defaults that define the category — by their absence

What we refuse to build.

  • Spray-and-pray mass outreach without a valid consent edge. There is no "blast this list" button. If it cannot be consented, it cannot be sent. This will cost us deals against tools that ship that button; we keep the refusal anyway.
  • Bought or scraped contact databases resold as "candidate data." This is the data-decay trap the whole category is built on; our entire rediscovery thesis is the opposite — resolved identity over the customer's own perimeter, not a rented snapshot that rots.
  • Dark-pattern candidate manipulation — fake urgency, manufactured scarcity, opaque "you're a top match" nudges. The transparency surface is load-bearing; we will not undercut it.
  • Cross-customer talent-pool sharing or benchmarking. Deferred indefinitely, same as Recruiter — the privacy posture and the contracts don't support it and we have no plan to change either.
  • Engagement-score vanity metrics that reward volume of touches. We measure influence on hiring, not activity.
§ IV · The architectural advantage

Consent is an edge, not a checkbox.

Why Ember is different from a Gem-with-AI clone — and why "one graph" alone is honestly not the wedge.

Start with the honest disclaimer, because the diligence room will. "We unify talent on one graph" is not an open differentiator. Beamery has marketed a "talent graph" for nine years; Cornerstone trademarked "People Graph" in May 2026; Eightfold's whole pitch is a talent-intelligence graph. Our own research lead, Eglin, is explicit that the people-graph noun is contested and that leaning on it as the wedge is a losing position (Landscape, mid-2026). So we do not lean on it. Three things survive Eglin's comparative read, and Ember is built on those three.

One · Consent as a substrate property, not an application flag.

In every incumbent recruiting CRM, consent is a column: a flag on a contact record, enforced — if at all — in application code that a feature flag or a migration can quietly bypass. In Stratum, consent attaches to the edge, with scope, purpose, validity, issuer, and jurisdiction, at the storage layer (substrate, § VI). A nurture campaign is a graph traversal that cannot expand across an edge whose consent does not cover the campaign's purpose and jurisdiction. Compliant nurture is not a setting you remember to switch on; it is the only kind of traversal the engine can perform. For a category whose dominant legal risk is GDPR and CAN-SPAM exposure in automated outreach, this is the defensible core no competitor can retrofit without rebuilding from the data model up.

Two · Rediscovery over a resolved, cross-system identity.

The pure-play CRMs hold a talent pool as a static snapshot — a list of contacts that begins decaying the moment it is built, because the same person exists three times across the ATS, the old CRM, and a spreadsheet, and changes jobs without telling anyone. Ember's pool is a query over a graph whose identities are reconciled continuously across the customer's whole perimeter (entity resolution at τ = 0.93; Forge, Post 01). The silver medalist who has since moved companies is still the same node; the referral who is also a past applicant is one person, not two. The pool does not rot, because it is not a list — it is a live view.

Three · The candidate-side transparency the category forgot.

Recruiter already refuses the black-box match score; Ember points that same explanation layer at the candidate. A person can see where they stand, what data is held, what consent they have given, and revoke it in one place. No incumbent built this, because their architecture treats the candidate as a record to be marketed to, not a party to a relationship. Ours treats them as a node with consent edges they control. The ethics and the architecture are the same fact.

And, in the house pattern: every product extends the eval set before it ships (Console added identity-conflict probes; Recruiter added bias probes; Cairn added trajectory-coherence). Ember adds two — consent-validity probes (does this proposed outreach have a valid consent edge for its purpose and jurisdiction?) and nurture-fairness probes (is the AI prioritizing or de-prioritizing whom to nurture along a protected-class proxy?). Kernel co-owns these with the CHRO advisory panel.

§ V · The pricing model

Priced on relationships kept, not seats filled.

The incumbents charge per recruiter seat — which taxes you for growing the team doing the work. We don't. That is a wedge, not just a preference.

The pure-play recruiting CRMs price per seat, and seats are expensive — which means a customer scaling its sourcing function pays linearly more to do more of the thing the tool exists to enable. Stratum has never priced per seat (Pricing), and Ember will not start. It follows the house model: a platform fee plus a usage tier on the outcomes that matter — qualified, consented conversations and rediscovery-influenced hires — with bundle economics that route customers toward the funnel they already half-own.

§ VI · The competitive positioning

Where we win, where we lose, and the names we will say in the diligence room.

The pure-plays are real and well-funded. We are honest about what they do better, and precise about the one thing they cannot do.

This is the firmer half of the document. Eglin's battlecards cover the talent-intelligence platforms (Eightfold, Phenom, Beamery, Cornerstone); a verified live-research pass covers the pure-play sourcing CRMs (Gem, hireEZ, Findem, SeekOut). The pattern across all of them is consistent: they have spent a decade making the recruiter's outreach faster, and almost none making it consented, deduplicated, or transparent to the candidate. That is the seam.

And the timing is ours. The whole category spent 2025–2026 racing to ship agentic sourcing — Beamery's "Ray," hireEZ's semi-autonomous "Agentic AI" (March 2025), Findem's "Copilot for Sourcing" and "Fia," Eightfold's interviewer and companion agents — straight into a regulatory regime that classifies recruitment AI as high-risk under the EU AI Act (penalties to €35M / 7% of turnover) and liability-bearing under NYC Local Law 144. They are pointing autonomous outreach at candidates precisely as the law makes consent, auditability, and explanation mandatory. A nurture engine that is compliant by construction is not a feature in that environment; it is the thing the buyer's General Counsel asks for by name.

vs Gem
The category leader for mid-market recruiting CRM and sequencing. Gem's LinkedIn/email sequencing, Chrome-extension sourcing, and analytics are mature and loved, and it has added its own AI Sourcing; we will not match that polish in v1. Its consistent buyer knock is price — it is expensive, and per-seat. We win where the pain is data decay and compliance, not sequencing speed, and on the per-seat-vs-outcome price model. We lose the team that just wants the best megaphone today, and we say so.
vs Beamery
Markets itself as "the leading talent CRM," ships silver-medalist rediscovery and a unified internal-mobility + external-sourcing model, runs the agent "Ray," and holds a genuine third-party bias audit (Holistic AI) — stronger than the category usually credits. We do not out-graph Beamery on vocabulary; it has owned "talent graph" for nine years. We differentiate on consent-on-the-edge as a substrate property and governance-as-published-artifact (the eval set, the decisions ledger), which Beamery does not ship. We lose large-enterprise TA-transformation RFPs in Year One; a Beamery battlecard is due within 30 days.
vs hireEZ / Findem / SeekOut
AI-sourcing breadth on large external corpora, now going agentic and well-funded. hireEZ ships semi-autonomous "Agentic AI" (Mar 2025); Findem raised a $51M Series C (Oct 2025) on a 1.6-trillion-data-point cloud with its "Fia" agent; SeekOut pairs deep search with an internal-mobility surface (Grow). Their open-web reach is wider than ours and always will be. Our counter is exactly that breadth's weakness: a rented, decaying external snapshot versus a resolved identity over the customer's own perimeter, plus a consent posture a scraped-data model structurally cannot offer. We win the compliance-exposed buyer; we lose the pure top-of-funnel volume play.
vs Eightfold / Phenom
Talent-intelligence platforms — broad, heavy, enterprise. Eightfold's 1.6-billion-trajectory graph is real and Fortune-500-proven; its consistent buyer complaint is cost and complexity. Neither publicly documents cross-system identity resolution into the customer's internal stack, or per-edge consent. We are lighter, consent-native, and transparent to the candidate; they are broader and more entrenched. We lose the large-enterprise "one suite" buyer and win the customer who wants the funnel on a substrate they can audit.
vs Paradox
Conversational automation (Olivia) — a different shape: an assistant that moves candidates through, not a relationship substrate. We can coexist, and we compete only on the candidate-experience claim, where transparency-by-architecture beats chat-by-default. Not a primary threat to the Ember thesis.
Where we win

Compliance-exposed 1,000–8,000 EE companies with a real sourcing function, who already trust a Stratum surface, who have been burned by data decay and a deliverability or GDPR scare, and whose CHRO wants to be able to prove consent in an audit. For them, "compliant by construction" and "the pool that doesn't rot" are not features — they are the reason to switch.

Where we lose

High-volume pure sourcing teams who measure success in messages sent and want the widest external reach and the most mature sequencing today. Also large-enterprise talent-graph RFPs where Beamery/Eightfold incumbency and breadth win. We will not chase either in Year One, and the plan is honest that Ember is a wedge product, not a category-share play.

§ VII · The evaluation plan

Six weeks to a decision, not six weeks to a launch.

The plan's output is evidence for the standalone-vs-module call, not a GA date. We build the cheap core, put it in front of real sourcers, and let adoption decide.

Wk 1 T+26d → T+33d Ratify · scope · name

Chairman read on § X. Launch-ahead-of-demand, the name, and standalone-vs-module resolved before a line of build. If the Chairman prefers module-first, the plan flexes to that without rework — the core is the same.

Scope frozen to the v1 list in § III. Tessera takes the naming review. Two design partners selected from the existing cohort (Mercator and Halcyon are the candidates — both have a sourcing function and an acute compliance posture).

Wk 2–4 T+33d → T+54d Build the cheap core

Forge ships the three load-bearing pieces on the existing graph: dynamic talent pools (queries), identity-resolved rediscovery (reuse of entity resolution), and the consent-gated nurture traversal. No new storage layer — this is the architectural thesis being proven or falsified in code.

The candidate transparency surface and "why this person" reuse Recruiter's explanation layer. Kernel adds the consent-validity and nurture-fairness probes to the eval set before any outreach is enabled.

If the core is not assembling cheaply by T+47d, that is itself the answer: Ember is not "mostly assembly," RISK-FOCUS-008 fires, and we fold it into Recruiter as a feature rather than stand up a line.

Wk 5–6 T+54d → T+68d Design-partner test · live

Mercator and Halcyon run Ember against one real pool each — a population of past finalists and referrals — with consent-gated nurture live. Vector shadows every session; weekly feedback on the Recruiter cadence.

The joint consent-data audit runs here: we prove, on a customer tenant, that no outreach left without a valid consent edge. That audit artifact is the single most important output of the six weeks.

The falsification test: do sourcers use it unprompted, and does the consent-coverage hit 100%? If sourcers don't adopt, we have our answer and we do not ship a fifth product on conviction alone.

T+68d decision gate Standalone · module · or shelve

Three doors, decided on evidence. Standalone product line (if adoption + standalone-logo interest are both real); module inside Recruiter (Vector's preferred path, if adoption is real but standalone pull is not); or shelved into the Recruiter backlog (if sourcers didn't bite).

The recommendation goes to the Compact with the adoption data and the consent-audit artifact attached. The Chairman holds the final call on whether Stratum carries a fifth product line. Helm's "last new surface" condition (R-018) is in force regardless of which door we walk through.

§ IX · What could go wrong

Four risks, and the one we are most exposed to.

R-018 is the one Helm flagged and the one we most respect: the fifth product that starves the four.

R18NEWfocus risk · Helm owns

A fifth product line dilutes the four that are working.

MitigationHonest-cost constraint and a hard cap. Ember gets one agent + Vector's bandwidth for six weeks, and only because the thesis is that it is assembly over existing infrastructure. If it isn't assembling cheaply by T+47d, we fold it into Recruiter and do not stand up a line. Helm's "last new surface before consolidation" condition is binding on the whole company, not just Ember.

R19NEWdemand risk · Vector owns

We built it on conviction and the sourcers don't actually adopt it.

MitigationThe entire § VII plan is a falsification test, not a launch. The >60% unprompted-adoption gate at T+68d exists precisely to kill the product cheaply if the conviction was wrong. We would rather spend six weeks learning "no" than six months shipping into silence.

R20NEWliability risk · Forge owns

A nurture campaign misfires and we become the deliverability/compliance story we sell against.

MitigationConsent coverage is a 100% hard gate, enforced at the substrate, not a target. The consent-validity eval probes run before any outreach is enabled, and the joint customer audit at T+68d proves the gate held on a real tenant. We refuse the spray-and-pray button that creates this risk in the first place (§ III).

R21NEWpositioning risk · Compass owns

The "talent graph" noun is contested and we get heard as me-too.

MitigationWe do not lead with "one graph." Per Eglin's landscape read, we lead with the three claims that survive comparison: consent-on-the-edge, resolved-identity rediscovery, and candidate transparency. The Beamery battlecard (due 30 days) sharpens the vocabulary contrast so we are never reduced to "talent graph, but newer."

§ X · Open to the Chairman

Three questions for your read.

The agents recommend evaluation, not launch. These three calls the Compact reserves to you — and the first is the real one.

Question 01 · the real one

Do we build a product ahead of the demand, on architectural conviction — or do we wait for the chorus?

Every prior product had a customer pull. Ember does not — it has a deferral coming due, a soft beta signal, and Forge's conviction that the expensive part is already built. The agents propose a six-week, one-agent falsification test as the disciplined way to act on conviction without betting the company on it. The question for you is whether Stratum should ever build ahead of demand at all, or whether that is a discipline we are not yet entitled to. We think the cheap, falsifiable test is exactly how a company earns that right. Your call.

Question 02 · the name

Stratum Ember — approve, or send back to Tessera?

The thesis behind the name: a candidate relationship is an ember — kept warm, never left to go cold (the anti-ghosting position), ready to reignite the moment a role and a person's readiness align (rediscovery). It pairs tonally with Cairn and Tenure. It is provisional; Tessera holds naming review, as she did when she renamed the agent surface to Console. We need a yes/no/send-back by T+33d so the design partner contracts carry the right word.

Question 03 · the live disagreement

Standalone product line, or module inside Recruiter? (Vector dissents toward module-first.)

This is the one genuine disagreement in the document. Compass sees a standalone wedge with its own buyer (the sourcer) and its own brand position (candidate transparency). Vector argues module-first: ship Ember's core inside the Recruiter beta, prove sourcers adopt it, and avoid the cost and cannibalization of a fifth line until the evidence demands one. The agents agree the T+68d data should decide — but you may want to set the default now. Our recommendation: let the adoption test rule, with module-first as the honest fallback.

Recruiter hires the person in the pipeline. Ember is how you earn the right to the thousand who aren't in it yet — and never let them go cold.
