02 · Thesis
“Most vendors call it a graph. A few have built one. The difference is whether the edge exists when no human writes a JOIN.”
Eglin · Industry research desk · Stratum, Inc. · 2026-05-22

This presentation walks the full case: definition, 70 years of historical accident, an honest 12-vendor scan, why agents need a graph, four buyer vignettes, the architectural argument, compliance, honest counter-arguments, and what it means for Stratum.

03 · Definition

What a people graph actually is.

The identity-resolved, time-aware, multi-source representation of every person connected to a company — employees, candidates, contractors, alumni — together with their skills, attestations, relationships, trajectories, and consents, expressed as a labeled property graph queryable by traversal. Five properties matter.

  • Identity-resolved One human = one node, across every system that holds a record about her. Maria in Workday, M. Lopez in Greenhouse, mlopez@ in the directory — one node, not four rows.
  • Time-aware Every edge carries a valid-from and valid-to. Headcount on any historical Tuesday is a query, not a backup.
  • Multi-source Ingests from HRIS, ATS, LMS, payroll, equity, directory. If the HRIS is replaced, the graph survives.
  • Consent-bound Edges carry consent metadata. Traversal is governed, not just indexed.
  • Queryable as a graph Shortest-path, neighborhood, and subgraph queries run as graph traversals at interactive latency — not fifteen-table JOINs.
04 · What it is not · part 1

Not the HRIS. Not the ATS. Not the org chart.

  • Not the HRIS An HRIS (Workday, SuccessFactors, Oracle HCM) is a system of record for badged employment, payroll, and position data. Single-source by design. It is not the answer to who is this person, across everything we know about them?
  • Not the ATS funnel An ATS (Greenhouse, Lever, iCIMS) is a directed-acyclic state machine over a candidate’s movement through stages. Rich about the funnel; poor about everything after the offer. The ATS→HRIS handoff, in every implementation studied, is a re-key: the same person gets a new ID and the application-to-employment link is severed.
  • Not the org chart An org chart (ChartHop, Pingboard) is one dimension of the graph — the reports-to edge — drawn at a single point in time. A people graph contains the org chart. The org chart does not contain the people graph.
05 · What it is not · part 2

Not LinkedIn. Not Microsoft Graph.

  • Not LinkedIn LinkedIn’s Economic Graph is the closest thing to a real people graph at population scale — 1 billion members, 41,000 skills, 67 million companies. But it is the external graph. It does not know who is on Tuesday’s payroll. It cannot be queried by a manager asking who on my team is at flight risk. A people graph is the internal counterpart: same structure, bounded by employment, governed by the company’s consent regime.
  • Not Microsoft Graph Microsoft Graph is an identity-and-collaboration graph over mail, calendar, files, and Teams. The “person” in Microsoft Graph is an Entra ID, not an entity-resolved human across HCM systems. In 2026, every HCM people graph will need to interoperate with Microsoft Graph; few will be subsumed by it.

The single-sentence summary: a people graph is what you have when the same human being is one node across every system that knows them, every edge carries a time interval and a consent flag, and the structure is queryable by traversal.

06 · The historical accident · era 01 of 04
Era 01

Payroll-first.

1950s — 1980s

ADP was founded in 1949 as Automatic Payrolls, Inc., processing timesheets on punch cards. The data model was a tape, indexed by File Number. A “person” was a row with hours, a deduction stack, and a check address.

Nothing about skills. Nothing about relationships. Nothing about time other than the current pay period. This model worked because the question was: cut the right check by Friday. It was answered by a row.

Every architectural decision made in this era optimized for payroll throughput. The row model was the right answer to the question being asked. It was also the founding accident that the next 70 years would build on top of.

07 · The historical accident · era 02 of 04
Era 02

HRIS-of-record.

1990s — 2000s

PeopleSoft, Oracle HRMS, and SAP HR generalized the payroll row into a richer employee record: position, supervisor, cost center, benefits enrollment. Hundreds of tables, thousands of foreign keys. A “person” was now multiple rows across multiple tables, joined at query time.

A real improvement — you could ask questions across position, history, and benefits — but it committed the industry to a paradigm: the person is what the JOIN says they are. If the JOIN is wrong, the person is wrong. If two tables disagree, the more senior team wins.

08 · The historical accident · era 03 of 04
Era 03

Cloud HCM suite.

2010s

Workday (IPO 2012) and SAP SuccessFactors (acquired 2011) re-platformed the HRIS onto the cloud. The marketing pitch was one system for the employee lifecycle.

The reality: the suites absorbed core HR, payroll, benefits, time, and basic talent modules — and left everything else (ATS, LMS, engagement, performance, equity) to specialists. The suite became the system of record for the badged employee. Everything outside the badge was a separate system, integrated by event-bus or by weekly CSV.

The data model improved. The silo problem got worse, because now there were more silos and the suite vendor had a commercial incentive to make integration to non-suite systems harder.

09 · The historical accident · era 04 of 04
Era 04

Point-solution explosion.

2010s — present

Greenhouse took the ATS out of the suite. Lattice took performance and engagement. Cornerstone took learning. Carta took equity. Each was a categorical improvement over the suite module it replaced. Each was also another database with another identity scheme. The average mid-market HR stack: 7 to 12 systems-of-record about people.

Halcyon Health, with 2,400 employees, has 12,112 rows across seven systems (Forge’s Essay No. 02). Halcyon is closer to median than to outlier.

Each era left behind an architecture correct for its decade and structurally incorrect for the next one. The question being asked now is only answerable by a graph.

10 · Who is doing this · shipped

12 vendors. Shipped vs. claimed.

Shipped means primary-source evidence of a real graph data model with entity resolution and traversal in production. Partial means parts are real but identity resolution or time-awareness are not demonstrated. Claim means graph language in the marketing; no primary evidence beyond the brochure.

Shipped — 4 vendors
  • Gloat Loomra · talent marketplace
    ShippedGraph
    Workforce knowledge graph, semantic embeddings, skills inference. Eglin rates shipped for the workforce-context use case. Not a full HCM substrate; the talent-marketplace layer atop one.
  • Beamery Talent Lifecycle Management
    ShippedSemantic web
    Explicit semantic-web knowledge graph, reportedly 17 billion data points. Most graph-native of any recruiting-focused vendor. Constraint: it is a recruiting graph; once hired, the customer’s HRIS takes over.
  • LinkedIn Economic Graph
    ShippedDistributed graph DB
    The reference architecture. Tens of TB, ~500K QPS, documented in a 2018 SIGKDD paper. The external graph — not a people graph for your company — but sets the engineering bar.
  • Microsoft Graph · Viva
    ShippedIdentity graph
    Real, large, public-API graph over Entra identity, mail, calendar, files, Teams. Not a people graph in the HCM sense — identity is the Entra account, not an entity-resolved human — but a foundational substrate every HCM graph will need to interoperate with.
11 · Who is doing this · partial

Parts of the architecture are real.

Partial — 3 vendors
  • Workday Skills Cloud · Career Hub
    PartialRow-store
    The skills layer is real and well-engineered — a spatial representation of skills with implicit skill discovery. The underlying HCM remains a relational object model. If you are inside Workday end-to-end, the skills layer is useful. The graph stops at the suite boundary.
  • Eightfold AI Talent Intelligence Platform
    PartialGraph + ML
    Deep skills-inference layer trained on a very large external talent corpus; architecture appears graph-and-embedding. Eglin’s open question: how is identity resolved across customer-internal systems once the candidate becomes an employee? Publicly available materials do not answer this clearly.
  • ChartHop People analytics + AI
    PartialRow-store + AI
    Consolidated data store where HR, job, and business fields live in one flexible model. The integration story is real; the architecture is a consolidated warehouse with a chart visualization layer, not a graph.
12 · Who is doing this · claimed

Graph language. No primary evidence.

Claim — 5 vendors · 1 out of scope
  • HiBob Bob · modern HRIS
    ClaimRow-store
    No primary documentation of a graph data model found. Row-store HRIS with strong UX.
  • Rippling Employee Graph
    ClaimRow-store
    The “Employee Graph” branding describes workflow-orchestration features, not a graph traversal layer. Strongly-normalized relational model with consistent identity inside the Rippling tenant — useful, but does not resolve identity from outside Rippling’s data plane.
  • Lattice Performance + HRIS
    ClaimRow-store
    Row-store HRIS with strong workflow primitives. No graph storage model documented.
  • Eqtble / Deel Analytics · Global payroll
    ClaimWarehouse-backed
    Both use graph-adjacent language in marketing; no primary architecture documents found. Warehouse-and-dashboard reality in both cases.
  • Mercor AI hiring → labeling pivot
    Out of scope
    Raised at $10B in Oct 2025, then pivoted from enterprise hiring to supplying domain experts to AI labs. A category exit, not a contender.
13 · The strategic argument

Why agentic companies need a people graph.

An agent queries a substrate, reasons over the result, and produces a decision. The quality of every step depends on the quality of the substrate. If the substrate is a stack of tables, the agent has to do its own JOIN. That JOIN is where the failures live.

  • Decision provenance

    An agent that touches clusters, not rows, produces a defensible decision trail — the bedrock of EU AI Act Article 13 and NYC AEDT audit obligations. Without a graph, provenance is best-effort.

  • Cross-system reasoning

    An internal mobility recommendation requires recruiting interactions, current role, peers, learning history, and stated preferences. A graph turns this into one query. A row-based stack turns it into multi-system orchestration with N partial answers.

  • Consent enforcement

    Consent applies to edges, not systems. In a graph, the consent flag is on the edge; traversal honors it by construction. The agent does not have to know the policy — the policy is in the substrate.

  • Time-aware queries

    Flight-risk analysis needs trajectory: engagement trend, project-assignment shifts, comp-adjustment timing. In a graph, trajectory is a property of the node, indexed for traversal.

  • Connector-economy unlock

    MCP and the broader connector economy assume the agent reasons over data from systems the agent vendor did not build. The substrate that makes that reasoning coherent is a graph.

14 · Why humans get a better experience · vignette 1 of 4
Manager experience

“Tell me who on my team is at flight risk and why.”

Row-based stack Engagement scores in Lattice. Comp history in the HRIS. Project assignments in the project tool. 1:1 notes in Notion. Exit-interview data in yet another system, if it exists at all. The manager assembles the picture herself — thirty minutes, twice a year, or she does not assemble it at all.
People graph One question, one traversal. The agent walks from the manager to each direct report, then to each report’s engagement edge, comp-history edge, peer-departure neighborhood, and project-allocation edge — with confidence scores and a citation trail the manager can interrogate. Same question, answered in seconds.
15 · Why humans get a better experience · vignette 2 of 4
Employee experience

“The company knows me, without making me re-onboard every quarter.”

Row-based stack New manager? Re-enter your goals. New project? Re-state your skills. New benefits window? Re-confirm your dependents. Every system is the first system, every quarter. The employee experience on a row-based HCM is one of being asked, repeatedly, to tell the company things it should already know.
People graph The employee’s node already contains her skills (inferred + stated, with provenance), trajectory (projects shipped, learning completed, moves made), relationships (peers and managers worked with), and stated preferences. New surfaces query the node; they do not re-collect.
16 · Why humans get a better experience · vignette 3 of 4
Recruiter experience

“The candidate I just spoke to was an employee here three years ago. I should know that before the next call.”

Row-based stack A boomerang candidate shows up in the ATS as a brand-new record. The recruiter learns about prior employment from a hand-typed note on the resume, or from a LinkedIn search done after the interview. The ATS and HRIS are different databases with different identity schemes — the structural reason internal mobility has been unsolved for a decade.
People graph The boomerang is a single node with two employment edges, one closed and one in flight. The recruiter sees the prior tenure, role, manager, reason-for-leaving, and rehire eligibility — all before the next call. No stitching. No guessing.
17 · Why humans get a better experience · vignette 4 of 4
Compliance experience

“Prove this decision was not biased. Prove this deletion request was honored everywhere.”

Row-based stack Decision provenance is reconstructed post-hoc, badly. Deletion is a manual reconciliation across N systems with N retention policies. Neither question is answerable in a defensible window without heroic manual effort.
People graph Decision provenance is a structural property of the edge the decision touched. Deletion is a bounded subgraph delete — every edge attached to the deleted node, in every system, with confirmation. The auditor’s question is a single bounded subgraph query.
18 · The architectural rationale

Why a graph beats a warehouse for this problem.

Warehouse paradigm
  • Rows + foreign keys
  • JOIN at query time
  • Time = SCD2 / effective dates
  • Consent = row-level security
  • Identity = source key
  • Agent = doer of JOINs
  • Best for: batch analytics, known query shapes, finance + ops reporting
Graph paradigm
  • Nodes + edges
  • Traversal at query time
  • Time = edge property
  • Consent = edge property
  • Identity = resolved cluster
  • Agent = reasoner over neighborhoods
  • Best for: interactive agentic queries, open-ended traversals, people-systems-of-understanding

The graph does not replace the warehouse. Both exist, in every Stratum customer, fed by the same ingestion pipeline. The architectural mistake is using the warehouse alone for questions that are structurally graph-shaped. — See Forge’s Engineering Post / people-graph-substrate for the implementation specifics.

19 · Compliance

Provenance lives on edges, not on rows.

The graph is not a compliance feature. It is a compliance substrate. Three regulatory frames make this concrete.

NYC Local Law 144 (AEDT)
Annual bias audit required. Candidate notification 10 days before use.

In effect since July 2023. The NY State Comptroller’s Dec 2025 audit found enforcement “ineffective” — 75% of 311 calls misrouted; 17 potential violations found by auditors vs. 1 by DCWP. 2026 enforcement posture is substantially more rigorous. Decision provenance must be defensible and reproducible. A graph stores it as a structural property of the edge.

EU AI Act · High-risk HR
Effective August 2, 2026. Fines up to €15M or 3% of global turnover.

Mandatory risk management, data governance, documentation, human oversight, and continuous monitoring for AI systems used in recruitment, screening, performance prediction, and most material HR decisions. Applies extraterritorially. The data-governance requirements presume you can produce a defensible description of the data the system reasoned over, with provenance and consent. A row-based stack reconstructs. A graph stores.

US state patterns (California SB 1001, AB 331, Colorado AI Act, the pay-transparency wave) push the same direction: the deployer must explain the individual decision. Provenance, consent, and time are all on the edge. The auditor’s question is a bounded subgraph query.

20 · Where the thesis is weakest

The honest counter-arguments.

A research brief that does not name where its thesis is weakest is marketing in a research jacket.

  • “Entity resolution is hard, and most companies don’t have clean enough source data to make a graph work.”

    This is correct, and it is the strongest counter-argument. The hardness is also the moat. Vendors who treat it as a feature ship a fake. Vendors who treat it as the product ship a real one.

  • “The incumbents say they already have a graph. Are they really wrong?”

    Partially. Each incumbent graph stops at a boundary — the suite boundary, the talent-acquisition boundary, the skills-ontology boundary — that is structurally upstream of the question buyers are asking. Your incumbent has a graph over a slice; the people graph is the substrate that connects all the slices.

  • “A deeply integrated graph creates lock-in. The row store is at least easier to leave.”

    Legitimate. The right mitigation is data portability at the layer below the graph: the customer’s ingested source data and entity-resolution decisions are exportable, in open formats, on demand.

  • “Schema rigidity is the failure mode of graphs at enterprise scale.”

    Real concern. The right architecture: a small core ontology (person, role, relationship, attestation, consent, decision) with customer-specific edge types as typed extensions. Schema discipline is part of the product.

21 · What this means for Stratum

Three Tier-2 implications.

Eglin’s recommendations to Helm and Compass for Tier-2 review. Not commitments — the Compact decides.

Implication 01 · Positioning
Stop saying “MCP-native HCM.” Start saying “the people graph.”

CHROs and CIOs do not buy a protocol. They buy a substrate that produces a different experience. MCP is the connector story underneath; the people graph is the headline.

Implication 02 · Engineering
Publish the resolver eval-set numbers, on the record, by end of Q3.

The differentiation move is to publish what the incumbents cannot: precision-recall curve on adversarial cases, conflict-queue throughput, contractor-to-employee transition accuracy. Forge already has the numbers.

Implication 03 · Partnerships
A graph-of-graphs interop position with Beamery, Gloat, and Microsoft Graph.

Don’t compete head-on with the specialized graphs. Position Stratum as the layer that integrates them — graph-of-graphs interop, with MCP as the wire protocol.
