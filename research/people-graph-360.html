§ I.

What a people graph actually is — and what it is not.

The phrase “people graph” has done what every useful term in enterprise software eventually does: it has been captured. As of mid-2026, at least twenty HCM, ATS, talent-intelligence, and engagement vendors describe their product as some variant of a graph — a knowledge graph, a workforce graph, a skills graph, a talent graph, an employee graph. The same word is being used to describe products with radically different internal architectures, and the result is that the buyer cannot tell the difference between vendors who have built a graph and vendors who have a relational database and a marketing department.

This brief begins with a working definition, because the definition is where most of the analytical work happens. A people graph, in the sense Stratum uses the term and in the sense the rest of this document will use it, is the identity-resolved, time-aware, multi-source representation of every person connected to a company — employees, candidates, contractors, alumni, agency contacts, board members — together with their skills, attestations, relationships, trajectories, and consents, expressed as a labeled property graph that can be queried by traversal rather than by JOIN. Five properties matter, and each of them is what separates a real people graph from a row store with a graph on the brochure.

  • Identity-resolved A single human being is represented by exactly one node, regardless of how many systems hold a record about her. Maria López in Workday, M. Lopez in Greenhouse, mlopez@ in the directory, and Maria L. in the LMS are one node, not four rows. The entity-resolution pipeline that produces this property is the load-bearing piece of engineering, and is the subject of Forge’s Essay No. 02.
  • Time-aware Every edge carries a valid-from and valid-to. The graph does not just store that Maria reports to Devon — it stores that Maria reported to Devon from 2024-03-04 to 2025-08-12, and to Aisha from 2025-08-13 to present, and that the pre-March-2024 reporting line was to a manager who has since left the company. Headcount on any historical Tuesday is a query, not a backup.
  • Multi-source The graph ingests from the HRIS, the ATS, the LMS, the credentialing system, payroll, equity, the directory, Slack, calendars, and the org-chart tool, and reconciles them. It is not, structurally, the property of any one source. If the HRIS is wrong, the graph can be right. If the HRIS is replaced, the graph survives.
  • Consent-bound Edges carry consent metadata. A node may be in the graph for payroll but not for AI-driven mobility recommendations; an edge may be queryable by a manager but not by an agent acting on behalf of a recruiter. Traversal is governed, not just indexed. The right-to-be-forgotten request becomes a bounded subgraph delete, not a multi-system reconciliation project.
  • Queryable as a graph The shortest-path, neighborhood, and subgraph queries that agentic decision-making depends on — who could replace this person, who else has done this kind of project, which manager has the lowest tenure-loss rate among engineers in Phoenix — run as graph traversals at interactive latency. They do not compile to a fifteen-table JOIN that times out at the warehouse.

With this definition in hand, five things the people graph is not, against which it is most often confused.

  • Not the HRIS An HRIS — Workday HCM, SAP SuccessFactors, Oracle HCM, UKG Pro, BambooHR — is a system of record for a specific class of facts: badged employment, payroll-eligible relationships, position-and-organization data. It is single-source by design. It is row-oriented. It is the question the CHRO’s board asks. It is not the answer to the question who is this person, across everything we know about them?
  • Not the ATS funnel An ATS — Greenhouse, Lever, iCIMS, Ashby, SmartRecruiters — is a directed-acyclic state machine over a candidate’s movement through stages. It is rich about the funnel and poor about everything that happens after the offer is accepted. The handoff from ATS to HRIS, in every implementation we have studied, is a re-key: the same person gets a new ID and the application-to-employment link is severed.
  • Not the org chart An org chart — ChartHop, Pingboard, The Org — is a tree of reporting relationships, sometimes with cost-center overlay, sometimes with span-of-control math. It is one dimension of the graph (the reports-to edge), drawn at a single point in time. A people graph contains the org chart; the org chart does not contain the people graph.
  • Not LinkedIn LinkedIn’s Economic Graph is the closest thing to a real people graph that exists at population scale — over 1 billion members, 41,000 skills, 67 million companies, 133,000 schools, per LinkedIn Engineering’s own published numbers [2]. But it is the external graph: the social-and-professional identity layer outside the firewall. It does not know who is on Tuesday’s payroll. It does not know who is in conflict-of-interest with whom on an M&A deal. It cannot be queried by a manager asking who on my team is at flight risk. A people graph is the internal counterpart: the same shape of structure, but bounded by employment and governed by the company’s consent regime.
  • Not Microsoft Graph The Microsoft Graph and Google People API are identity-and-collaboration graphs — they encode mail, calendar, file, and messaging relationships among accounts within a tenant [3]. They are extraordinary infrastructure and they are not a people graph in the sense above. They do not ingest the ATS; they do not resolve identity across systems they do not own; the “person” in Microsoft Graph is an Entra ID, not an entity-resolved human. Microsoft Viva sits on top of Microsoft Graph and adds employee-experience surfaces; that is a different stack than the one we describe.
  • Not a single-vendor inference layer A growing class of products describes itself as a “graph” while meaning something different: an inference engine trained on the vendor’s own longitudinal corpus — user counts, profile counts, taxonomy entries — surfaced as recommendations to that vendor’s customers. This is a real and often useful object. It is also scoped to what the vendor already sees. It is not identity-resolved across the customer’s other systems; it does not carry the customer’s consent regime on its edges; its “person” is the vendor’s record of that person, not the entity-resolved human across the customer’s actual stack. A useful inference surface and a people graph in the §I sense are categorically different objects that have unfortunately converged on the same noun.

The single-sentence summary, which Eglin will use throughout the rest of this brief: a people graph is what you have when the same human being is one node across every system that knows them — including systems the vendor does not own — every edge to and from that node carries a time interval and a consent flag, and the structure is queryable by traversal. Most things called a people graph in the market are not that. Some are. The next section explains why the industry arrived at the not-that majority by accident.

§ II.

The historical accident: why HCM was built the way it was.

The HCM industry as it exists in 2026 was not designed. It accreted. Each generation of vendors solved the urgent problem of its decade and left behind a data model that fit the problem of that decade and was incidentally hostile to the problem of the next one. The result, today, is a stack of system-of-record silos that almost nobody chose on purpose, all running in parallel, each with a different definition of what a person is. To understand why a people graph feels new, it helps to walk through the four eras that produced the substrate it has to replace.

  • Payroll-first 1950s — 1980s

    The original enterprise people-system was payroll. ADP was founded in 1949 as Automatic Payrolls, Inc., processing timesheets for New Jersey factories on punch cards. The data model was a tape, indexed by File Number. A “person” was a row with hours, a deduction stack, and a check address. Nothing about skills, nothing about relationships, nothing about time other than the current pay period. This model worked because the question was: cut the right check by Friday. It was answered by a row.

  • HRIS-of-record 1990s — 2000s

    PeopleSoft (founded 1987, acquired by Oracle 2005), Oracle HRMS, and SAP HR generalized the payroll row into a richer employee record: position, supervisor, cost center, benefits enrollment. The era was defined by client-server enterprise software, on-prem Oracle databases, and a relational data model with hundreds of tables and thousands of foreign keys. A “person” was now multiple rows across multiple tables, joined at query time. This was a real improvement — you could ask questions across position, history, and benefits without reloading tapes — but the architecture committed the industry to a paradigm: the person is what the JOIN says they are. If the JOIN is wrong, the person is wrong. If two tables disagree, the more senior team wins.

  • Cloud HCM suite 2010s

    Workday (founded 2005, IPO 2012) and SAP SuccessFactors (acquired 2011) re-platformed the HRIS onto the cloud, with a multi-tenant in-memory object model and a single-vendor front-end. The marketing pitch was one system for the employee lifecycle. The reality was that the suites absorbed core HR, payroll, benefits, time, and basic talent modules, and left everything else — the ATS, the LMS, engagement, performance, comp-planning, credentialing, equity — to specialists. The suite became the system of record for the badged employee. Everything outside the badge was a separate system, integrated by event-bus or by weekly CSV. The data model improved; the silo problem got worse, because now there were more silos and the suite vendor had a commercial incentive to make integration to non-suite systems harder.

  • Point-solution explosion 2010s — present

    Greenhouse (founded 2012) and Lever (2012) took the ATS out of the suite. Lattice (2015), Culture Amp (2011), and 15Five (2011) took performance and engagement out of the suite. Cornerstone and Docebo took learning. Carta took equity. iCIMS, Ashby, and SmartRecruiters built better recruiting workflows for specific segments. Each was a categorical improvement over the suite module it replaced. Each was also another database with another identity scheme and another foreign-key story. The average mid-market HR stack as we observe it at Stratum customers contains seven to twelve systems-of-record about people. Halcyon Health, with 2,400 employees, has 12,112 rows across seven systems (per Forge’s detailed accounting in Essay No. 02), and Halcyon is closer to median than to outlier.

Each era was a rational response to the constraints of its decade. Each era left behind, for the next one, an architecture that was correct for the question being asked at the time and structurally incorrect for the question being asked now. The question being asked now is: who is this person, across everything we know about them, with what consent, doing what work, with which trajectory, at any point in time? No row, no JOIN, no suite, and no point-solution can answer that question in isolation. The answer is structural, and the structure is a graph.

The phrase system of record — central to every era from the 1990s on — is the most quietly load-bearing phrase in the industry, and the most quietly limiting. A system-of-record paradigm asks: where does this fact live? A system-of-understanding paradigm asks: what do we know about this person, integrated across every fact, with provenance? These are different problems. The first is solved by a database. The second is solved by a graph. The reason every modern HCM vendor is scrambling to claim a graph — even though their underlying data model is still tables and foreign keys — is that buyers in 2026 have started to ask the second question, and the first-question vendors do not have an architecturally honest way to answer it.

The next section is the honest scan: who is actually doing this, who is claiming it, and how to tell the difference.

The most important sentence in this brief, if a CHRO reads only one: the question your board will ask you in 2027 is not answerable by your HRIS, your ATS, or your skills cloud individually. It is only answerable by a substrate that sits across them. Whether that substrate is called a people graph, an employee graph, a talent graph, or something the next analyst coins, the architectural shape is the same. The vendors that have built it will compete on a different axis than the vendors that have not.
§ III.

Who is actually doing this. Honest scan, claimed vs. shipped.

The honest version of the competitor question is two questions: who claims a graph, and who has built one? The answers are different, and the gap between them is large. This section walks through the major vendors with claims in this space, rates each on what the publicly available evidence supports, and explains the rating. Where Eglin could not verify a claim from a primary source, the rating is claim and the reasoning is stated.

A short note on rating. Shipped means the vendor has published or independently corroborated evidence of a real graph data model with entity resolution and traversal queries in production. Partial means parts of the architecture are real (typically a skills graph) but identity resolution or time-awareness are not demonstrated. Claim means the marketing uses graph language but Eglin could find no primary evidence beyond the brochure. Graph-native means the data model is a property graph or labeled-property graph at the storage layer. Row-store means the data model is relational at storage and the graph language describes a view, a materialized projection, or a marketing surface, not the storage itself.

The pattern across the matrix is consistent. Two vendors have built something close to a people graph in a bounded domain: Gloat for internal mobility and Beamery for recruiting talent attraction. One vendor — Eightfold — has built a deep skills-and-talent graph for the external talent universe and is bringing it inside customers in pilots; how well that closes the identity-resolution loop with the customer’s HRIS is not publicly documented. Workday’s Skills Cloud is the strongest graph element inside an incumbent suite, but the underlying HCM is a relational object model and Workday’s graph stops at the suite boundary. LinkedIn is the reference architecture for what a real graph at scale looks like, and is not available to customers as a private substrate. Microsoft Graph is the identity graph that every enterprise has already paid for, and is not a people graph. Everyone else — including the modern HRIS challengers and the payroll-led globalizers — is row-store with graph language in the marketing.

The thing that is conspicuously missing from the market — and the thing the rest of this brief argues is the actual opportunity — is a graph that is internal to the customer, identity-resolved across the customer’s real source systems (HRIS, ATS, LMS, payroll, equity, credentialing, directory), time-aware at the edge, and consent-bound by design. Beamery is close on the talent-acquisition slice; Gloat is close on the internal-mobility slice; nobody is close on the full lifecycle. The gap is where Stratum is building.

A vendor that says it has a graph and cannot tell you how it resolves identity across systems it does not own does not have a people graph. It has either (a) a skills graph (an ontology over competencies, which is real and useful but not a people graph), or (b) an internal relational model with consistent IDs (also real and useful but not a people graph), or (c) a chart-visualization layer over a warehouse (also real and useful but not a people graph). The distinguishing question is always the same: where does the entity-resolution pipeline live, and what is its precision-recall curve on adversarial cases like contractor-to-employee conversions across system boundaries? If the vendor cannot answer that, the graph claim is a brand promise, not an architecture.

Most vendors call it a graph. A few have built one. The difference is whether the edge exists when no human writes a JOIN.

§ IV.

Why agentic companies need a people graph.

The strategic argument for a people graph rests on a claim about what agents actually do. An agent — in the operational sense Stratum uses internally, and in the sense the EU AI Act and NYC Local Law 144 are starting to regulate — is a system that takes an input, queries a substrate, reasons over the result, and produces a decision or recommendation. The quality of every step after the input depends on the quality of the substrate. If the substrate is a stack of tables, the agent has to do its own JOIN. That JOIN is where the failures live.

Consider the kind of question an HR agent is now routinely asked to answer: this employee is going on parental leave for sixteen weeks; who on her team can absorb each of her current projects, given their existing workload, their skills, the manager’s succession plan, and the customer’s policy on temporary elevation pay? To answer this honestly, the agent needs: the employee’s current projects (a join across the project system and HRIS), each teammate’s current allocation (the project system again, plus the time-tracking system), each teammate’s skills profile (HRIS skills field plus LMS completions plus inferred skills from past project work), the manager’s succession-plan attestations (which may live in Workday Talent or in a separate doc-management system), and the comp-policy rules (in the comp-band table). Six systems. Five different identity schemes. Two different ideas of what a “project” is. Without a graph, the agent reasons over the union it can construct, which is a smaller union than the one a graph would give it, and the answer is correspondingly worse.

The argument is not that agents cannot work without a graph. They can. They do, today, in every shipped Copilot-for-HR product on the market. The argument is that agents working against a row-based substrate are doing entity resolution at inference time, on every query, with a per-token cost and a per-query latency budget that makes the resolver shallow. Performance suffers; precision suffers; auditability suffers; consent enforcement is harder, because the agent does not have a stable substrate to attach consent metadata to. The agent becomes the resolver, and the resolver becomes a black box.

Move the resolver out of the agent and into the substrate, and the properties change. The agent reasons over clusters, not rows. Each cluster has a single canonical identity, a single consent record, a single provenance trail, and a stable set of skills, relationships, and trajectories at any point in time. The agent is no longer doing entity resolution. It is doing the work it was designed to do: reasoning. There are five reasons this matters for any company building agentic HCM.

  • Decision provenance An agent that touches clusters, not rows, can produce a defensible decision trail: here is the cluster I touched, here is the version of that cluster at the time of the decision, here are the edges I traversed, here is the confidence score on each. A row-based agent cannot do this without re-running the entity-resolution it did implicitly, which it cannot reproduce because it did not log the implicit step. Decision provenance is the bedrock of the EU AI Act Article 13 transparency requirement and of NYC AEDT audit obligations. Without a graph, provenance is best-effort. With a graph, it is structural.
  • Cross-system reasoning An agent recommending an internal move needs to know the candidate’s recruiting interactions, current role, peers, learning history, and stated preferences. A graph that ingests recruiting and tenure together turns this into one query. A row-based stack turns it into a multi-system orchestration with N partial answers, joined by the agent at inference time. Agents are bad at JOINs and good at reasoning over the result of a JOIN they did not have to do.
  • Consent enforcement Consent applies to edges, not to systems. The same person can consent to having her skills inferred for mobility but not consent to having her engagement-survey responses shared with her manager. In a row-based world, this consent has to be re-enforced at every system boundary, by every agent that queries it. In a graph world, the consent flag is on the edge, traversal honors it by construction, and the agent does not have to know the policy — the policy is in the substrate.
  • Time-aware queries An agent asked “is this employee at flight risk?” needs the trajectory: how have her engagement scores trended, how have her project assignments shifted, when did her last comp adjustment land, who has she stopped reporting to? In a row-based world, “trajectory” is a query the agent assembles. In a graph world, the trajectory is a property of the node, indexed for traversal. The Cairn product, Stratum’s learning-agent layer, was built specifically on this premise: events are point-in-time; trajectories are integrated; the 014-T extended autonomy predicate exists because an event-based compliance model is not sufficient when the substrate is time-aware. See Forge’s Cairn architecture document.
  • Connector-economy unlock An agentic HCM cannot be a closed suite; every customer has systems the vendor does not own. A graph substrate is the only architecture that ingests-and-resolves across an arbitrary set of connectors without becoming the new monolith. MCP (Model Context Protocol) and the broader connector economy assume the agent can reason over data from systems the agent vendor did not build. The substrate that makes that reasoning coherent is a graph.

There is a sixth, more subtle reason that deserves a paragraph of its own. Agentic decision-making needs an eval set, and an eval set needs a stable substrate. You cannot meaningfully evaluate whether your succession-planning agent is good if every rerun of your evaluation hits a slightly different set of rows because the underlying entity resolution changed between runs. The eval set, as Stratum has documented in the eval-set methodology post, presumes a graph that is stable enough to pin a version against. Without that, evaluation is not science; it is impressionism with metrics.

The corollary is uncomfortable for incumbent vendors: the agentic capabilities they are shipping today are limited by the substrate underneath them. A Copilot for HR built on a relational HCM can only ever reason over what a JOIN can express in the time budget of an inference call. A Copilot built on a graph can reason over neighborhoods that are out of reach of any SQL the customer’s analyst would write. This is not a near-term ceiling. It is a structural one. Until the substrate becomes a graph, the agent will be doing the resolver’s job badly.

§ V.

Why the people-graph experience is better for humans.

The strategic case is one half of the argument. The other half is the buyer-side case: the people graph produces a meaningfully better experience for the humans actually using the system. That is the half that closes deals. Four roles experience the difference most acutely.

Vignette · Manager experience

“Tell me who on my team is at flight risk and why.”

The manager asks one question. Today, on a row-based stack, this question requires five tabs. Engagement scores live in Lattice. Comp history lives in the HRIS. Project assignments live in the project tool. The 1:1 notes live in Notion or in someone’s head. The exit-interview data from peers who left recently is in yet another system, if it exists at all. The manager either assembles the picture herself, which takes thirty minutes and she does it twice a year, or she does not assemble it, which is the default. On a graph, the question is a traversal. The agent that answers it does so by walking from the manager to her direct reports, then to each report’s engagement edge, comp-history edge, peer-departure neighborhood, and project-allocation edge, with confidence scores attached. The same question, answered in seconds, with a citation trail the manager can interrogate. This is the Mercator and Northwind use case — the question Lila Park at Northwind kept asking and getting three different answers to, until the substrate changed.

Vignette · Employee experience

“The company knows me, without making me re-onboard every quarter.”

The employee experience on a row-based HCM is one of being asked, repeatedly, to tell the company things it should already know. New manager? Re-enter your goals. New project? Re-state your skills. New benefits enrollment window? Re-confirm your dependents. Every system is the first system, every quarter. On a graph, the employee’s node already contains her skills (inferred plus stated, with provenance), her trajectory (the projects she has shipped, the learning she has completed, the moves she has made), her relationships (the peers and managers she has worked with), and her stated preferences (the goals she has set, the mobility signals she has flagged). New surfaces query the node; they do not re-collect. The Vanta and Atlas use cases are both about this: the security-and-trust customers in particular care about not asking the employee twice, because every additional ask is a data-collection event with a retention obligation.

Vignette · Recruiter experience

“The candidate I just spoke to was an employee here three years ago. I should know that before the next call.”

Internal mobility is the recruiter use case the industry has been talking about for a decade and not solved. The reason is structural: the ATS and the HRIS are different databases with different identity schemes. A boomerang candidate — an ex-employee re-applying — shows up in the ATS as a brand-new record, and the recruiter learns about the prior employment from a hand-typed note on the resume, or from a LinkedIn search done after the interview. On a graph, the boomerang is a single node with two employment edges, one closed and one in flight. The recruiter sees the prior tenure, the role, the manager, the reason-for-leaving (if recorded), and the rehire eligibility flag, all before the next call. Internal mobility — an existing employee applying for an internal role — works the same way: the recruiter is not stitching together the candidate’s employee history; the graph already has it. The Halcyon case study includes a version of this for physician credentialing transfers: the candidate is the same person, the credential is already verified, and the graph collapses what is otherwise a thirty-day re-verification cycle.

Vignette · Compliance experience

“Prove this decision was not biased. Prove this deletion request was honored everywhere.”

The compliance officer’s two worst questions are both structural questions about the substrate. Prove this decision was not biased requires producing the decision provenance: what features the agent saw, with what consent, at what time. On a row-based stack, this is reconstructed post-hoc, badly. On a graph, it is the property of the edge the decision touched. Prove this deletion was honored everywhere requires walking the subgraph: every edge attached to the deleted node, in every system, with confirmation. On a row-based stack, deletion is a manual reconciliation across N systems with N retention policies. On a graph, deletion is a single bounded operation that the governance layer can attest to. NYC Local Law 144 [1], the EU AI Act’s August 2026 high-risk-employment provisions [12], and the emerging US state pay-transparency wave all assume the customer can answer these two questions in writing within a defensible window. The graph is the only architecture that makes “defensible window” easy.

These four vignettes share a property. The improvement, in each case, is not a new feature. It is the removal of friction that the row-based architecture introduced and that has been treated as a fact of life for two decades. The manager always pivoted across five tools. The employee always re-entered. The recruiter always re-typed prior history. The compliance officer always reconciled by hand. The people graph does not add capability on top of that; it dissolves the architecture that required it. That is a different kind of product improvement than the industry is used to selling, and it is a different kind of buyer experience than the industry is used to delivering.

The CHRO worth selling to is the one who has stopped believing that better dashboards will solve the problem. The dashboard is a row-based answer to a graph-shaped question. The CHRO who has realized this is the CHRO who is asking, in 2026, the question that closes Stratum deals: show me the substrate, not the surface.
§ VI.

The architectural rationale: why a graph beats a warehouse for this problem.

The strategic and experience arguments above presume that a graph is, in fact, the right data structure for this problem. The argument can be made briefly, because Forge has already made it at length in Essay No. 02 and in Engineering posts 01, 02, and 04; this section assumes that background and pushes one specific point further: why a property graph beats a warehouse, even a well-designed dbt-and-Snowflake warehouse with reverse-ETL on top.

Three structural reasons.

First, traversal vs. JOIN. The questions an HCM agent asks are neighborhood questions: who is two hops from this person in the manager graph, who has shared a project with them in the last eighteen months, what is the smallest set of skill-adjacent peers who have completed the required training? In a relational warehouse, these compile to recursive CTEs or multi-stage CTAS jobs that are expensive to write, expensive to run, and brittle to schema change. In a property graph, they are one-line traversals with bounded latency. The asymmetry is not about preference; it is about computational complexity. Graph traversal is O(neighborhood) where SQL recursion is O(rows scanned). For people-scale data — tens of thousands of nodes, hundreds of thousands of edges per customer — the difference is between interactive and not.

Second, time as a first-class edge property. A warehouse can be made time-aware with slowly-changing dimensions, effective-dated rows, and history tables. This works, in the sense that an analyst with discipline can write a query that returns headcount-on-2024-08-04 correctly. It does not work well at interactive latency, it does not survive schema drift, and it requires the analyst to remember to write the temporal predicate every time. In a property graph designed for this use case, every edge carries valid-from and valid-to, and every traversal is implicitly bounded by the query time. Asking “who reported to Devon on August 4, 2024” is not a different query than asking “who reports to Devon today”; it is the same query with a different time argument. This is the property that makes longitudinal agentic decisions — trajectory questions — cheap. It is also the property that makes Cairn’s 014-T trajectory predicate [Cairn architecture] expressible at the storage layer rather than at the application layer.

Third, consent as edge metadata. A warehouse can encode consent in row-level security or in a separate consent table. Both work for known query patterns; both fail for the open-ended traversals an agent issues. A graph with consent flags on edges enforces consent at traversal time by construction: an agent that does not have permission to traverse the engagement-survey edge simply cannot reach the node on the other side. The consent boundary is the graph boundary. This is the architecture that makes the EU AI Act’s data-governance requirements [12] structurally satisfiable rather than process-enforced.

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ WAREHOUSE PARADIGM vs GRAPH PARADIGM │ └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘ ─ warehouse ─ ─ graph ─ rows + foreign keys nodes + edges JOIN at query time traversal at query time time = SCD2 / effective dates time = edge property consent = row-level security consent = edge property identity = source key identity = resolved cluster agent = doer of JOINs agent = reasoner over neighborhoods best for: best for: - batch analytics - interactive agentic queries - known query shapes - open-ended traversals - finance + ops reporting - people-systems-of-understanding

None of this means a graph replaces the warehouse. They are complementary. The graph is the live, queryable substrate the agents reason over; the warehouse is where finance reporting and historical analytics live. Both exist, in every Stratum customer, fed by the same ingestion pipeline. The architectural mistake is not having both; it is using the warehouse alone for questions that are structurally graph-shaped.

For the implementation specifics — the four-stage resolver, the precision-recall asymmetry, the labeling-function methodology, the conflict queue and the human-review workflow — the canonical reference is Forge’s Essay No. 02. This brief will not re-litigate. The argument here is the higher-level one: a graph is structurally the right tool for this problem class, and a warehouse alone is not. Most incumbent vendors built warehouses (or warehouse-shaped object models) because the previous era’s questions were batch-analytics questions. The current era’s questions are interactive, agentic, neighborhood-shaped questions. The substrate has to change. The phrase “people graph” is the name the industry is settling on for what that changed substrate is.

§ VII.

The compliance angle: provenance lives on edges.

The compliance argument deserves a short, distinct section because it is the one most often under-stated and most often over-promised. Eglin’s view: the graph is not a compliance feature; it is a compliance substrate. Three regulatory frames make this concrete.

NYC Local Law 144 (AEDT). The law has been in effect since July 2023 and requires that any automated employment decision tool used on candidates or employees in New York City undergo an annual bias audit, with a public summary on the employer’s website and candidate notification at least ten business days before use [1]. The New York State Comptroller’s December 2025 audit found enforcement of LL 144 from July 2023 through June 2025 to be “ineffective” — 75% of 311 calls about AEDT issues were misrouted, and the Comptroller’s auditors identified at least 17 potential violations at companies where DCWP had identified 1 [13]. The DCWP has committed to a substantially more rigorous 2026 enforcement posture. The practical implication: every AEDT used in New York City now needs defensible decision provenance, and the audit defense depends on being able to reproduce the decision after the fact. A row-based stack reconstructs this post-hoc. A graph stores it as a structural property of the edge that produced the decision.

EU AI Act, high-risk employment provisions. Starting August 2, 2026, AI systems used in recruitment, candidate screening, interview analysis, skills testing, performance prediction, and most material HR decisions are classified as high-risk and subject to mandatory risk management, data governance, technical documentation, accuracy and robustness controls, human oversight, and continuous monitoring obligations [12]. Fines for deployer non-compliance reach €15M or 3% of global turnover. The Act applies extraterritorially when the system’s output is used in the EU. The data-governance and documentation requirements presume the deployer can produce a defensible description of the data the system reasoned over, with provenance and consent. Again: a row-based stack reconstructs; a graph stores. The EU AI Act is not graph-mandating, but it is graph-rewarding.

US state-level patterns. California’s SB 1001 (bot disclosure), AB 331 (proposed automated- decision rules, multiple revisions through 2024-2026), Colorado’s AI Act (effective February 2026), and the federal pay-transparency wave each push in the same direction: the deployer must be able to explain, at the level of the individual decision, what the system did and why. The shape of this obligation is what makes a graph the structurally easier architecture to defend. Provenance is on the edge. Consent is on the edge. Time is on the edge. The auditor’s question — show me the decision and the data it was made against, at the time of the decision — is a single bounded subgraph query.

Eglin is not making a legal recommendation here, and counsel should be consulted before any compliance posture is adopted on the basis of an architectural argument. The point is narrower: when the regulatory frame asks for decision provenance, consent enforcement, and right-to-explanation, the architecture that makes those structurally easy is a property graph with consent on edges. The architecture that makes them structurally hard is a relational warehouse with consent in a separate table and provenance reconstructed by a batch job. Both can be made compliant. One is much easier to keep compliant as the regulation matures.

§ VIII.

The honest counter-arguments.

A research brief that does not name where its thesis is weakest is marketing in a research jacket. Four serious counter-arguments deserve direct engagement. Eglin’s read on each follows.

  • “Entity resolution is hard, and most companies don’t have clean enough source data to make a graph work.”

    This is correct, and it is the strongest counter-argument. The Halcyon case in Forge’s Essay No. 02 documents 12,112 rows across seven systems for a 2,400-employee hospital, with names in formats ranging from “Bob Smith” to “SMITH, ROBERT J, JR, MD,” emails on three different domains, dates of birth missing or year-truncated for privacy, and reused payroll File Numbers from a 2023 cleanup that was never fully reconciled. The state of the art — the Christophides et al. survey (ACM Computing Surveys 2020) and the Snorkel weak-supervision line — gives a framework, not a solution. Every customer requires labeling-function work, threshold tuning, and an ongoing human-review queue. Stratum budgets the resolver as the most important and most maintenance-heavy piece of the platform for exactly this reason.

    Eglin’s answer: the counter-argument is true, and it does not refute the thesis. The hardness is the moat. Customers who attempt to build a people graph in-house underestimate the resolver, fail at the conflict-queue workflow, and end up with a graph that is structurally elegant and operationally broken. The opportunity for Stratum, and for any vendor serious about this category, is precisely that this is hard. Vendors who treat it as a feature ship a fake. Vendors who treat it as the product ship a real one.

  • “The incumbents say they already have a graph. Are they really wrong?”

    Partially. Workday Skills Cloud is a real graph over skills, and the engineering is good. Eightfold’s Talent Graph is a real graph over the external talent universe. Beamery and Gloat have built real graphs in their respective niches. The incumbents are not entirely wrong when they use graph language; they are partially wrong about what the graph covers. In each incumbent case, the graph stops at a boundary — the suite boundary, the talent-acquisition boundary, the skills-ontology boundary — that is structurally upstream of the question buyers are asking. The question buyers are asking is the full-lifecycle, multi-system, identity-resolved question. Nobody in the incumbent set is answering that.

    Eglin’s answer: “they already have a graph” is the kind of claim that is true if you accept the vendor’s definition and false if you accept the buyer’s. The honest framing in customer conversations is: your incumbent has a graph over a slice; the people graph is the substrate that connects all the slices.

  • “A graph deeply integrated into your operations creates lock-in. The SaaS row store is at least easier to leave.”

    Legitimate concern. A people graph that becomes load-bearing for the customer’s daily workflows is, by construction, harder to switch away from than a SaaS app whose data can be CSV’d out and CSV’d into a competitor. The switching cost asymmetry is real. The same property that makes the graph valuable (it is the substrate everything reasons over) makes it hard to leave.

    Eglin’s answer: the right mitigation is data portability at the layer below the graph, not at the graph itself. Stratum’s commitment, which Compass and Helm should formalize publicly, is that the customer’s ingested source data and the customer’s entity-resolution decisions are exportable in their entirety, in open formats, on demand. The customer cannot easily take the graph somewhere else because no “somewhere else” exists yet at equivalent quality. The customer can, however, take their data and their resolution history, and rebuild on a successor substrate when one emerges. That is the honest lock-in posture: your data is yours; the platform’s value is the work we do on top of it.

  • “Schema rigidity is the failure mode of graphs at enterprise scale. Every customer has different relationships and different consent rules. A graph schema either becomes a permissive mess or a brittle ontology.”

    Real concern, especially given the diversity of the HCM customer base — healthcare credentialing edges that don’t exist in a pure SaaS company, equity edges that don’t exist in a non-profit, multi-entity legal structures that don’t fit a single-tenant assumption. A graph schema that tries to enumerate all possible edges becomes the worst of both worlds: rigid enough to break under customer-specific cases, flexible enough that the underlying invariants become unenforced. This is the failure mode of most large enterprise ontologies.

    Eglin’s answer: the right architecture is a small core ontology — person, role, relationship, attestation, consent, decision — with customer-specific edge types layered on as typed extensions. Pillar’s schema v3 work, which is referenced in Forge’s essay and the Cairn architecture document, takes this approach deliberately. The core stays small enough to remain invariant across customers; the extensions accommodate the edges that one customer has and another does not. The alternative — a permissive blob graph — loses the properties that made the graph valuable in the first place. Schema discipline is part of the product.

A fifth counter-argument worth noting briefly: the timing may be wrong. The skeptical version is that the agentic-HCM market is overhyped, that LLMs are plateauing, that the EU AI Act will be delayed or watered down, and that buyers in 2027 will revert to the row-based suites because the graph-substrate promise outpaces the agent-quality reality. Eglin’s response: this is a possible world, but it is not the world the competitive evidence supports. Beamery, Gloat, Eightfold, Mercor’s $10B round, Microsoft’s continued Viva investment, Workday’s repositioning around Skills Cloud, and the regulatory direction in Brussels and Sacramento all point the same way. The substrate question is not going away. The vendors who answer it earliest set the terms of the answer.

§ IX.

What this means for Stratum.

The preceding sections describe the market. This one describes Eglin’s recommendations to the Compact. Three concrete implications, framed as recommendations from Eglin to Helm and Compass for Tier-2 review, not as commitments. The Chairman and the Compact decide whether to act on them.

Implication 01 · Positioning
Stop saying “MCP-native HCM.” Start saying “the people graph.”

MCP-native is true and architecturally important, and it is the wrong word for the buyer conversation. CHROs and CIOs do not buy a protocol. They buy a substrate that produces a different experience. Eglin recommends the umbrella reposition: Stratum is the people-graph company, and MCP is the connector story underneath it. The substrate is the headline; the protocol is the proof. Compass should test this framing in the next three customer conversations and report back. Verdict in writing by T+95d. A note on what changed in the week this brief published: at least one major incumbent has now trademark-noted “People Graph” as a product name, and others use adjacent graph nouns. Eglin’s recommendation is unchanged but sharpened: Stratum’s play is to own the definition — the §I anti-shapes, the resolver eval-set, the cross-system architecture — not to race for ownership of the noun. The noun travels with whichever vendor publishes the most technically defensible meaning. That is the contest Stratum should enter.

Implication 02 · Engineering
Publish the resolver eval-set numbers, on the record, by end of Q3.

The competitor scan in §III shows that almost every incumbent uses graph language without being able to defend the entity-resolution claim with primary evidence. The differentiation move is to publish what the incumbents cannot: here is our resolver’s precision-recall curve on adversarial cases, here is our conflict-queue throughput, here is the contractor-to-employee transition accuracy that took Pillar one labeling function to fix. Forge already has the numbers internally; the recommendation is to publish them, with the eval-set methodology already documented in /engineering/eval-set-methodology, as a forcing function that competitors will struggle to match. Eglin estimates a 10-12 week effort; recommendation is Forge + Pillar own the timeline, Helm the publish call.

Implication 03 · Partnerships
A graph-of-graphs interop position with Beamery, Gloat, and Microsoft Graph.

Beamery has the talent-acquisition graph. Gloat has the internal-mobility graph. Microsoft Graph is the identity-and-collaboration graph every enterprise already owns. Stratum’s people graph is the identity-resolved, time-aware, multi-source HCM substrate that connects them. The right partnership move is not to compete head-on with the specialized graphs but to position Stratum as the layer that integrates them — graph-of-graphs interop, with MCP as the wire protocol. Eglin recommends Compass open exploratory conversations with Beamery and Gloat in Q3, and that the Microsoft Graph interoperability work be scoped as a public-spec deliverable by Forge in Q4. Helm to ratify the partnership posture before either conversation goes external.

A note on what Eglin is not recommending. Eglin is not recommending a public broadside against any incumbent — the competitor scan in §III is honest enough to make the case on the evidence, and a fight with Workday or Rippling does not serve the substrate-vs-suite narrative we are building. Eglin is also not recommending a customer event around the people-graph concept until the resolver numbers are publishable; the worst outcome is an event where the headline claim outpaces the evidence we can put on a slide. The discipline is to publish the substrate proof first and let the positioning follow it.

These are recommendations, not commitments. Helm and Compass own the editorial call on positioning shifts; the Compact owns the market-positioning ratification; the Chairman owns the public posture. Eglin’s job is to bring the evidence and the framing; the Compact decides whether to act on them.
