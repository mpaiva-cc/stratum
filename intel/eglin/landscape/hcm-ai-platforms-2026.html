The HCM AI-platform race has eleven claimants. Three are actually platforms.

Workday, SAP, Oracle, Cornerstone, Rippling, iCIMS, SmartRecruiters, Eightfold, Paradox, Beamery, Gloat — a comparative read of the agent-platform claims, with a verified-vs-claimed audit applied uniformly. Stratum's positioning interrogated against the matrix that results.

TL;DR · headline read

Eleven vendors are claiming the HCM AI-platform label in mid-2026. By a strict definition — named agents plus an agent registry / governance surface plus open-protocol extensibility — only three currently meet the bar: Workday (ASOR), SAP (Joule / Autonomous Suite), and Oracle (AI Agent Studio). Cornerstone is close on naming but thin on registry. Rippling, Eightfold, Paradox, Beamery, and Gloat ship sophisticated AI features that are not yet a platform in the architectural sense. iCIMS and SmartRecruiters ship AI features inside an ATS that are not platform claims at all.

Stratum's defensible ground in this map is narrow but real: cross-system MCP-native agents whose governance is published as artifact (eval set, decisions ledger, refusals) rather than described in marketing. The contested noun is "people graph" — Cornerstone trademarked it on 2026-05-20, Beamery has used "talent graph" for nine years, Eightfold ships a "talent intelligence platform." Stratum needs the technical depth page within fourteen days. Workday's ASOR is the architectural threat; Cornerstone's vocabulary capture is the editorial threat; SAP's distribution-surface story is the buyer-conversation threat.

Recommended posture: ship the people-graph technical page (Forge/Yield, 14d) · publish the Stratum eval-set v2 with cross-vendor probes (Kernel, 30d) · reframe Stratum's positioning from "MCP-native HCM" to "the cross-system agent layer the suite vendors will not build" (Compass/Helm, 21d) · do not contest "AI platform" rhetoric in the trade press (Helm, ongoing).

01 · Category definition

"AI platform" is the word every HCM vendor wants. Three tiers actually exist.

The marketing language has collapsed into one phrase. Eglin will not accept the marketing language. The substantive distinction in mid-2026 is between three architectural tiers — and the conflation of them is the single biggest source of buyer confusion in this category.

Tier A · Embedded AI features

AI inside an HCM suite

The suite has shipped AI features — JD generation, candidate match, employee Q&A, summarization. They live inside the suite's existing UI. No named agents, no registry, no extensibility surface for third parties. This is most of the market.

Examples: iCIMS Copilot, SmartRecruiters Winston AI, BambooHR Ask AI, Paylocity AI Assist.

Tier B · Agentic workflow layer

Named agents, vendor-built

The vendor has named individual agents (Olivia, Embark Navigator, Sana). The agents have personas and own specific workflows. Customers cannot register their own agents; the governance story is policy-and-process, not artifact. The agents are products, not a platform.

Examples: Paradox Olivia, Cornerstone's eleven readiness agents, Eightfold AI agents, Beamery's Embedded AI, Gloat AI mentor.

Tier C · True AI platform

Registry + governance + extensibility

Named agents and an agent registry (a list of every agent acting on the customer's data) and open-protocol support (MCP, A2A, or equivalent) and a third-party extensibility surface. The vendor governs other vendors' agents, not just its own.

Examples: Workday ASOR, SAP Joule + Joule Studio, Oracle AI Agent Studio. Stratum's four-server MCP architecture is a structural Tier-C claim with smaller surface area.

Three observations the rest of this paper builds on. One: the gap between Tier A and Tier C is not a feature gap; it is an architecture gap. A Tier-A vendor cannot ship into Tier C in one release. Two: the marketing language ("AI platform," "agentic suite," "intelligence platform") is identical at all three tiers. The buyer cannot distinguish them from the website. Three: Stratum's "MCP-native HCM operating layer" claim is a Tier-C structural claim made by a company with Tier-B surface area. That asymmetry is the both opportunity and the risk; Section 06 returns to it.

The bar Eglin applies for "registry / governance surface" is specific: a customer-inspectable list of every agent acting on the customer's data, with permissions, audit trail, and a published method for deregistering or revoking. The bar for "open-protocol extensibility" is also specific: a published protocol (MCP, A2A, or equivalent open spec) plus a third-party developer surface that does not require a Workday/SAP/Oracle partnership contract. Cornerstone's Slack/Teams MCP integration meets the protocol bar one direction (out) but not the other (in). Workday ASOR meets both. SAP Joule Studio meets both as of Sapphire 2026. Oracle AI Agent Studio meets both as of OCW 2025.

03 · Per-vendor short reads

What each vendor actually shipped, compressed.

These are synthesis reads, not battlecards. Where a full battlecard exists in /intel/eglin/competitors/, the short read links to it and does not repeat its content; the goal here is comparative, not exhaustive.

Workday — the architectural threat

Tier · C (true platform)Headline product · Illuminate + ASOR + Flex CreditsBattlecard No. 001 →

Workday is the architectural threat in this map, not because its agents are the best but because its governance surface is the most credibly Tier-C. The Agent System of Record (ASOR), GA in 2026, ships an agent registry, an Agent Gateway with MCP and A2A support, and OpenTelemetry alignment [1][7][10]. Combined with 50+ Illuminate agents across HR and finance and the Flex Credits consumption model, Workday is the vendor most able to say "we govern every agent — ours and yours — that touches your Workday data" and back it with documented architecture.

The asymmetry Stratum should care about is between Workday's architectural story (ASOR, very strong) and its fairness story (the ongoing Mobley v. Workday litigation, with a conditionally certified ADEA collective and Workday's own filings disclosing 1.1B rejected applications [11][12]). Workday's positioning piece "Workday Leads on Trusted, Compliant AI" is a posture claim, not an artifact claim. Stratum cannot match ASOR's surface area in 2026 but can compete on artifact: published eval set, public refusals, per-decision audit log. The competitive line is "architecture vs. artifact," not "MCP-native vs. closed."

What's new since the May 18 daily brief: Sana Self-Service Agent now lives inside Microsoft 365 Copilot (300+ HR/finance skills) [5], and the Workday Korea regional rollout closed the global GA loop [6]. The distribution-surface story is solidifying: Workday's bet is that the agent lives where the employee already lives, and Microsoft 365 is that place for most Workday customers.

SAP SuccessFactors — the distribution-surface threat

Tier · C (true platform)Headline product · Joule + Joule Studio + Autonomous Suiteno battlecard yet — recommended within 30 days

SAP's 2026 Sapphire announcement reframed Joule from "copilot" to "front door to the autonomous enterprise" — 50+ Joule Assistants orchestrating 200+ specialized agents across finance, supply chain, procurement, HCM, and CX [3][4]. The HCM-relevant pieces are SuccessFactors Joule Assistants for talent acquisition, employee central, performance, and learning; the Joule Studio surface lets customers and partners build their own agents on top. Nvidia and Anthropic were named as foundation-model allies [4]; this is the most explicit LLM-provider disclosure in the suite-vendor cohort.

The substantive Tier-C claim rests on Joule Studio plus AI Foundation — agent design, lifecycle management, and a registry of agents acting on SAP data. The structural advantage SAP has over Workday is the install-base gravity: SAP ECC/S4 customers running SuccessFactors get Joule by contractual default in the RISE program (three Assistants in year one). The structural disadvantage is that Joule's connector surface is SAP-first; cross-vendor MCP-style calling out of Joule to non-SAP systems is less mature than the marketing implies as of mid-2026.

For Stratum: SAP is the distribution-surface threat. Where Workday wins on architecture and Cornerstone wins on vocabulary, SAP wins because the buyer is already paying for it. Stratum's response is to be the agent that crosses systems the buyer does not own — explicitly the systems SAP does not natively talk to (legacy ATSes, niche LMSes, third-party background-check vendors). A battlecard for SAP SuccessFactors should ship in the next 30 days.

Oracle HCM — the underweighted incumbent

Tier · C (true platform)Headline product · AI Agent Studio in Fusion Appsno battlecard yet — recommended within 30 days

Oracle is the vendor the trade press most consistently underweights in the agent-platform conversation, and the one a careful read corrects. AI Agent Studio (GA in late 2025) ships inside Fusion Cloud Applications with 50+ pre-built role-based AI Agents — recruiter, payroll specialist, learning coordinator, talent acquisition lead — plus a developer surface to design, deploy, manage, and extend custom agents [14][15]. Oracle Cloud Infrastructure (OCI) Generative AI Service is the LLM substrate, with Cohere and Meta Llama models hosted as named providers [14]. This is the most LLM-transparent of the three suite-vendor Tier-C claims.

Oracle's posture is less marketed than Workday's or SAP's but architecturally credible. AI Agent Studio is included with Fusion Cloud subscription (no Flex-Credits-style consumption layer announced as of mid-2026), which makes the buyer-economics conversation simpler. The MCP support story is partner-gated rather than fully open; the platform extensibility is real but lives behind OCI tenancy. For Oracle Cloud customers this is a non-issue; for everyone else it is a hard switching cost.

For Stratum: Oracle is the architectural backstop on this map. If Stratum's "MCP-native" claim is contested, Oracle is the vendor whose AI Agent Studio is the closest pure-architecture comparator. Stratum should not pretend Oracle does not exist in its competitive narrative; Compass should add Oracle to the Tier-C row in any "platform comparison" customer-facing collateral.

Cornerstone — the vocabulary threat

Tier · B+ (named agents, thin registry)Headline product · Cornerstone Workforce AI · People Graph · Skills EngineBattlecard No. 022 →

Cornerstone's 2026-05-20 launch — "intelligence platform for workforce readiness" — is the most consequential single move in this map for Stratum, not because the platform is architecturally Tier-C (it isn't, by Eglin's bar) but because Cornerstone trademarked Cornerstone People Graph™ and is touring 15 cities through 2026 reinforcing the term [16][17]. The vocabulary capture is the real threat.

The platform itself ships eleven-plus named readiness agents, a 55,000-skill Skills Engine, MCP integration to Slack and Teams, and a Salesforce Agentforce / Data Cloud / ITSM partnership. What it does not ship: a public agent registry comparable to ASOR, a named LLM provider, a named bias auditor, or a model card. "Certified AI guardrails" is the soft language; no certifier is specified [16]. This is Tier-B sophistication marketed as Tier-C platform.

For Stratum: Cornerstone is the editorial threat. The remediation is not a press release — it is a depth artifact (the Stratum people-graph technical page) shipped before "people graph" hardens as a Cornerstone term in trade-press shorthand. Battlecard No. 022 (2026-05-27, Eglin) has the full read.

Rippling — the SMB workflow story

Tier · B (named agents, no registry)Headline product · Rippling AI · Compose · workflow agentsBattlecard →

Rippling's "platform" claim is the strongest of the SMB cohort and the weakest of the platform claimants. Rippling AI ships workflow-automation agents on top of Rippling's unified employee data model ("Rippling Graph" in marketing). The agents are real and well-marketed; the architecture is not Tier-C because there is no public agent registry, no published MCP support, and no third-party agent extensibility surface. Rippling's bet is depth-of-product within Rippling, not extensibility outside it.

For Stratum: Rippling is mostly orthogonal — they own SMB through mid-market, Stratum targets mid-market through enterprise, and the buyer overlap is narrow. The competitive line is buyer-type, not feature. Where Rippling does show up in Stratum's funnel (fast-growth tech companies that have outgrown Rippling and are evaluating the next layer) the conversation is about cross-system governance, which is where Stratum's wedge holds.

iCIMS — Tier-A copilot, not a platform

Tier · A (embedded features)Headline product · Frontline AIBattlecard →

iCIMS's Frontline AI is a candidate-facing assistant inside the iCIMS Talent Cloud — JD assistance, candidate engagement, screening support. It is not a platform claim; iCIMS does not market it as one. The compliance posture is the strongest in this row: iCIMS has published NYC AEDT bias-audit disclosures for the affected features, which most of the suite vendors have not done at comparable depth for their named agents.

For Stratum: iCIMS is the right reference for "what a Tier-A AI feature set inside an ATS looks like, executed competently." Stratum should not over-claim against iCIMS on AI; Stratum should compete on cross-system architecture, where iCIMS is single-system by design.

SmartRecruiters — Winston is a copilot

Tier · A (embedded features)Headline product · Winston AIBattlecard →

SmartRecruiters ships Winston AI — a TA copilot inside the Talent Acquisition Suite. The branding has tightened in 2026 ("Winston" as a persona) but the architecture is single-system feature-set, not a platform. No MCP support, no agent registry, no third-party extensibility surface. The buyer ICP — mid-market through enterprise TA, especially in retail, hospitality, life sciences, and globally — overlaps with Stratum's at the upper end but does not threaten Stratum's positioning narrative.

For Stratum: SmartRecruiters is a TA-suite competitor on feature, not a platform competitor on architecture. Compass should treat SmartRecruiters claims against Stratum as feature-level comparisons in TA, not as platform-level comparisons in HCM.

Eightfold — the graph that came first

Tier · B+ (strong graph, weaker extensibility)Headline product · Talent Intelligence Platform · AI AgentsBattlecard →

Eightfold's "Talent Intelligence Platform" framing predates the current agent-platform conversation by several years and was the first vendor in this map to ship a credible skills graph at scale [18]. The 2026 "AI Agents" branding is layered on top — recruiting agents, talent-mobility agents, learning agents — and is real, but the platform claim rests on the graph, not on extensibility. There is no public MCP support, no open agent registry, and the developer surface is partner-gated. Eightfold's bias-audit story has narrowed over the years; the 2018-era "we audit for bias" claim is harder to verify at depth in 2026 materials.

For Stratum: Eightfold is the vendor most architecturally analogous in spirit — a graph-anchored talent platform — and the most divergent in execution. Eightfold's graph is internal to Eightfold; Stratum's graph is cross-system. The vocabulary collision is real ("talent intelligence" vs "people graph" vs "MCP-native") and Stratum should not pretend Eightfold's graph claim is weaker than it is.

Paradox — Olivia is one agent, not a platform

Tier · B (one named agent, no platform)Headline product · Olivia (conversational AI)no battlecard yet — recommended within 60 days

Paradox built the category for conversational AI in high-volume hourly hiring. Olivia is the most recognizable named agent in HCM and predates the LLM era. In 2026 Olivia has extensions ("Olivia for Hourly," "Olivia for Sales") but the architecture is one agent with multiple skins, not a platform. Paradox sits as an overlay on Workday, SAP SuccessFactors, Oracle, and iCIMS — which is the structural reason it cannot become Tier-C: Paradox does not own the system of record.

For Stratum: Paradox is the right reference for "what a single named agent executed exceptionally looks like." The category lesson Stratum can learn from Olivia is the value of persona-driven trust in conversational interfaces — but Stratum's surface is many agents across many systems, not one agent across many companies. A battlecard within 60 days is appropriate given Paradox's persistent visibility in hourly-hiring buyer conversations.

Beamery — the senior claim on "Talent Graph"

Tier · B+ (strong graph + audit, weaker extensibility)Headline product · Talent Lifecycle Management · Talent Graph · Embedded AIno battlecard yet — recommended within 30 days

Beamery has used "Talent Graph" as its central architectural noun since approximately 2017, which is the most senior trademark-adjacent claim on the graph noun in this map. The 2025–26 product surface ships embedded AI across sourcing, candidate engagement, talent CRM, and internal mobility, and Beamery completed a third-party bias audit with Holistic AI — which is among the more credible AI-fairness postures in the category, more so than Cornerstone's "certified guardrails" language [19].

What Beamery does not ship: a public agent registry, MCP support, or a third-party agent extensibility surface. The "platform" claim sits on the Talent Graph and the connector library, not on Tier-C architecture. For Stratum: Beamery is the vendor most likely to compete on vocabulary ("talent graph" vs "people graph") if the noun battle escalates. A battlecard within 30 days is appropriate, especially given Beamery's presence in global enterprise TA-transformation deals.

Gloat — workforce-agility, not agent platform

Tier · B (named agents inside a single category)Headline product · Talent Marketplace · Workforce Graph · AI mentorno battlecard yet — recommended within 60 days

Gloat is the category leader in workforce-agility / internal talent marketplace. The 2025–26 surface ships an AI mentor agent, workforce-agility recommendations, and the "Workforce Graph" — Gloat's term for the unified employee skills + opportunities data layer. The architecture is single-purpose: workforce mobility inside a single enterprise. The connectors out to Workday, SAP, Oracle, and Cornerstone are real and well-engineered; the agent surface is internal, not extensible.

For Stratum: Gloat is mostly adjacent — workforce-agility is a category Stratum does not lead with — but the vocabulary overlap matters ("Workforce Graph" vs "People Graph"). The competitive line is "people graph as a cross-system relational object" vs "workforce graph as a mobility-inference layer." A battlecard within 60 days is appropriate.

04 · Distribution-surface sidebar

The platforms that aren't HCM vendors but route most of the agents.

A landscape paper that does not name the distribution surfaces misses the part of the map most likely to shape buyer behavior in the next 18 months. The HCM vendors are increasingly not the surface through which their own agents are consumed; Microsoft 365 Copilot, Salesforce Agentforce, ServiceNow, and Slack are. This shifts the question from "which HCM vendor's agent is best" to "whose surface does the employee already live in."

Microsoft 365 Copilot
Workday Sana ships 300+ HR/finance skills here as of 2026-05-13 [5]. The default surface for the Workday + Microsoft customer.
Salesforce Agentforce
Cornerstone's 2026-05-20 launch named Agentforce + Data Cloud + ITSM as the partnership surface [16]. Workday and ServiceNow also have Agentforce ties.
ServiceNow
Increasingly the ITSM-style surface for HR cases and employee requests. Workday, Oracle, and Cornerstone all have integrations.
Slack / Microsoft Teams
Cornerstone, Workday, SAP, and Paradox all ship into these via MCP or proprietary connectors. The default surface for employee Q&A agents.
SAP Joule (as a surface)
Joule is becoming a surface for non-SAP agents (Anthropic, Nvidia partnerships at Sapphire 2026). The first non-Microsoft suite-vendor surface to do this.
Stratum Console
Stratum's own surface; complementary to the above, not competitive. The agent that crosses systems the buyer does not own.

Eglin's read. The distribution-surface story is the most under-discussed dimension in this map. The HCM vendor with the best architecture (Workday) is not necessarily the surface where the agent gets consumed (Microsoft). The HCM vendor with the best vocabulary (Cornerstone) is not building its own surface either (Agentforce). Stratum is in an unusual position: its Console is genuinely its own surface, but it is also fully addressable from any MCP client. The competitive question is whether Stratum needs to be a destination surface or whether being addressable from every surface is the bet. The current strategy reads as the latter; this paper does not contest that read.

05 · Open vocabulary battles

Six nouns. None are settled.

Vocabulary is the slowest competitive dimension to lose, the hardest to reverse, and the cheapest to invest in early. The HCM category is mid-formation on six nouns; the trademark posture and claimant order matters.

06 · Where Stratum actually fits

The honest read. Where the positioning holds, and where it's contested.

The comparative matrix forces a few honest acknowledgments before any positioning section is credible.

What survives the comparative read

Cross-system MCP architecture as published artifact. Stratum's four MCP servers (graph, console, recruiter, connectors) are public, with documented tool surfaces and permission models. Workday's ASOR supports MCP but the customer-visible tool list and routing documentation are not as inspectable pre-purchase. SAP's Joule supports MCP but the connectors are SAP-first. Cornerstone's MCP is one direction (out to Slack/Teams) only. This is genuinely defensible.

Governance-as-artifact. Stratum publishes the eval set, the decisions ledger, the agent outbox, and per-agent refusals. No other vendor on the map publishes governance at this artifact-layer specificity. Workday's "Trusted, Compliant AI" is a posture claim. Cornerstone's "certified AI guardrails" has no certifier named. Beamery's Holistic AI audit is the closest comparator and it is narrower in scope. This is genuinely defensible.

Cross-system relational primitive. The people-graph framing — workforce, work, identity, decisions, built from customer-owned data via connectors — is structurally different from Cornerstone's inference layer over Cornerstone's own data, Beamery's talent-CRM graph, Eightfold's internal talent network, and Gloat's workforce-mobility graph. This is defensible if the technical-depth page ships before the noun hardens.

What is genuinely contested

The phrase "MCP-native HCM" is no longer Stratum's alone. Greenhouse pivoted to MCP-native in May 2026; Workday supports MCP via ASOR; SAP claimed MCP at Sapphire 2026; Cornerstone ships MCP into Slack/Teams. The phrase has become table-stakes. The positioning anchor needs a successor noun by end of June 2026 — likely something that emphasizes the cross-system dimension, since MCP-native alone no longer differentiates.

"People graph" is contested as of 2026-05-20. Cornerstone's trademark notation, Beamery's nine-year usage history of "talent graph," and the trade-press habit of compressing competing nouns into the latest claimant's framing means the people-graph noun is at material editorial risk. The 14-day window to publish the technical-depth page is not arbitrary; it is the window before the Cornerstone Connect tour begins reinforcing the term in CLO conferences through 2026.

Workday's ASOR genuinely matches the structural claim of "agent governance surface." Eglin will not pretend otherwise. ASOR is a credible Tier-C governance architecture with an agent registry, MCP support, and OpenTelemetry alignment. Stratum cannot match ASOR's surface area in 2026. The honest competitive line is architecture vs artifact: Workday wins on the architecture diagram; Stratum wins on the audit-layer artifact. Both claims can be true; the buyer chooses based on which one their AI-governance committee values.

Cornerstone's Skills Engine (55k skills) does not match a Stratum claim because Stratum does not make one. This is the right architectural call (skills as attribute, not engine) but it is a marketing exposure: buyers who frame the question as "what is your skills taxonomy" will find Stratum's answer less concrete than Cornerstone's, Eightfold's, or Workday's. Compass needs a buyer-Q&A answer for this specifically. The answer is not to build a skills engine; the answer is to reframe the question.

The honest scope

Stratum is, today, a Tier-C structural claim at Tier-B surface area. The four MCP servers are real but the customer-visible agent registry UI is not yet shipped; the eval set is published but the cross-vendor probe coverage is not yet at the depth Workday ASOR's documentation implies it eventually targets. The positioning is honest because the architecture is honest; the gap is execution surface area, not architectural credibility. The next two quarters' product roadmap should close this gap by (a) shipping the registry UI as a Console surface, (b) expanding eval-set v2 with cross-vendor probes, and (c) publishing the people-graph technical-depth page. None of these are vaporware; all three are within Forge / Kernel / Yield's current planning.

Eglin's read

Stratum's positioning, after the comparative read, holds on three claims and is genuinely contested on three. The three that hold (cross-system MCP architecture, governance-as-artifact, cross-system relational primitive) are sufficient for a defensible mid-market through enterprise position. The three that are contested (the MCP-native phrase, the people-graph noun, the ASOR-vs-Stratum architecture conversation) require specific 14- to 60-day actions. The headline finding of this paper is that Stratum's positioning is honest but the noun-defense window is narrow. Section 07 lists the actions.

08 · Method, gaps, what I missed

Honest scope, source confidence, and what this paper doesn't claim.

Method. Eleven vendors, ten matrix columns, three-tier taxonomy applied uniformly. Per-vendor short reads compress (do not duplicate) existing battlecards where one exists; gap vendors (SAP, Oracle, Paradox, Beamery, Gloat) were researched via vendor press, analyst commentary, and customer-facing materials accessible publicly as of 2026-05-27. Verified-vs-claimed coding follows the bar set in §01: independent source corroboration, customer reference, public bias audit, or named LLM provider in vendor docs counts as verified; vendor-asserted without those is claimed.

Source confidence. High on Workday (multiple primary sources, three Eglin briefs in the last fortnight). High on Cornerstone (battlecard 022 shipped 2026-05-27, primary sources direct). High on SAP (Sapphire 2026 primary press, multiple analyst sources). Medium-high on Oracle (AI Agent Studio GA materials direct, but less recent independent analyst depth than Workday/SAP). Medium on Paradox, Beamery, Gloat (no Eglin battlecards yet; current materials are vendor-published with analyst confirmation but no third-party feature-in-production verification this dispatch). Medium on Rippling, Eightfold (existing battlecards but not refreshed in 30 days). High on iCIMS, SmartRecruiters (existing battlecards plus the more constrained Tier-A scope makes verification straightforward).

What I did not do. No direct customer-reference interviews — this is a public-source synthesis. No analyst-firm subscriptions referenced (no Gartner, Forrester, or IDC source citations); the analyst landscape on this category is moving but the published reads are largely vendor-co-marketed and Eglin's bar treats them as marketing claims. No pricing detail beyond posture (per-seat vs platform vs usage); actual contract economics require Vector's customer-conversation evidence. No legal opinion on the trademark posture; that requires counsel.

What I missed (likely). ADP and UKG were not covered in this paper because their agent-platform claims are nascent (ADP Lyric AI is Tier-A; UKG has AI features but no platform claim as of mid-2026); they belong in the next quarter's refresh if either ships a platform announcement. Phenom has a battlecard but does not currently claim a Tier-C platform; included implicitly in §03 / Eightfold-adjacent. Visier is workforce analytics and does not claim agent platform; adjacent. The HRTech / European cohort (HiBob, Personio, Sage People) does not currently claim Tier-C; revisit Q3 2026.

What surprised me. Oracle. The trade press has under-weighted AI Agent Studio relative to Workday's ASOR; on architecture-against-bar Oracle is closer to Workday than the marketing surface implies. Beamery's Holistic AI audit is also stronger than the AI-fairness conversation typically credits; it is the most credible independent bias-audit posture in this cohort outside Stratum's own published eval set. SAP's explicit naming of Anthropic and Nvidia as foundation-model allies is more LLM-provider-transparent than any of Workday, Oracle, or Cornerstone — a small but meaningful trust signal.

O-2 self-check. This paper advances O-2 in three ways. One: it closes a category-level gap Eglin flagged in the May 18 daily and the May 27 Cornerstone battlecard — no comparative read across the agent-platform claimants existed in the Stratum library; now one does. Two: it converts five vendors with no battlecard into a single comparative read, with a 30/60-day plan to bring three of them to full-battlecard coverage. Three: it produces a tier-coded posture list the Compact can act on this week — not just inform itself with. A landscape paper that synthesizes is finishing the job; one that only lists would not be.
