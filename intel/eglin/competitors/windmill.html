00 · Scope of this battlecard

Authorization · Helm · 2026-06-04

Helm authorized a partial battlecard for Windmill on 2026-06-04 with a specific three-section scope: (1) verified-vs-claimed architecture, (2) the four-layer model evaluated against what each layer would have to become to threaten Stratum's substrate claim, and (3) two named, falsifiable escalation milestones. No defensive content from Compass. No customer outreach. No board notification — Windmill does not clear that bar at L2 Watch.

The reason it is partial: Windmill's substrate-threat probability inside a 6–12 month window reads at roughly 12–15%; the positioning-threat probability — vocabulary pollution in the trade press around "context graph" / "workforce graph" — reads materially higher, at 45–55%. A partial battlecard sized to the positioning risk is the proportional response. If either of the §3 milestones fires, this becomes a full battlecard inside seven days.

Cross-link: the broader competitive-substrate read sits in the People Graph 360 §III matrix and the §XI.vi addendum (rev 1.2, 2026-06-03). Windmill appears in both. This battlecard is the operational artifact those research pieces pointed to.

§1 · Verified vs. claimed architecture

A graph noun. An evidence surface.

Windmill's primary architectural claim, on its website and in the 28 April 2026 PR Newswire release, is "the context graph for your people" — described as "a continuously updated, cited understanding of the workforce" where "every insight traces back to a specific Slack thread, pull request, or piece of documentation" [1][2]. The claim is well-formed at the level of product positioning. The underlying architecture, as publicly stated, has none of the load-bearing properties a substrate would need.

Verified from primary sources

  • $12M seed led by Inspired Capital (Alexa von Tobel, MP), with Primary Venture Partners, Founder Collective, Oceans Ventures participating. Closed 28 Apr 2026 [1][2].
  • Founders: Max Shaw (CEO, formerly SVP Product at Yext), Brian Distelburger (co-founder of Yext), Mark Tanner (previously VP Strategy at Saturn) [1][6][7].
  • Shipping product since November 2025: AI-drafted performance reviews. 100+ customers including Kalshi, Rho, and Merge [1][2].
  • Integrates with Slack, GitHub, Google Workspace, and "30+ other tools where work already happens" [1][2].
  • Four named layers in the context-graph framing: People ("who they are and how they connect"), Evidence ("what actually happened"), Expectations ("what good looks like"), Perspectives ("what people think") [1][3].
  • Access surfaces stated in the release: a chat agent ("Windy"), a web app, and "full MCP and API support" [1].
  • Product surface today: Performance Reviews, 1:1s, Continuous feedback, Calibrations, Pulse surveys [3].
  • Claimed outcomes: "reviews completed 90% faster with 93% employee satisfaction" — vendor-published, not independently verified [1].

Claimed without architectural substance

  • "Context graph" as the noun for the system — no public material describes a graph database, a property-graph store, or a graph query layer. The four "layers" are content categories, not storage primitives.
  • Cross-system identity resolution — not described in any public material. The platform reads from Slack, GitHub, Google Workspace; whether and how a Maria López identity is resolved into a single canonical node across those sources is not stated.
  • Time as a first-class property of edges — absent from public material. Source-tagged provenance ("every insight traces to a specific Slack thread") is timestamped at the evidence layer, but the graph is not described as time-aware in the way a temporal property graph would be.
  • Per-edge consent metadata — absent from public material. No published consent model, no opt-in/scope framework, no policy layer described at the storage level. The release does not mention SOC 2, ISO 27001, GDPR posture, or any compliance certification.
  • "Continuously updated, cited understanding" — positioning, not architecture. Consistent with a RAG pipeline over the connected sources, surfaced through an LLM chat agent.
  • No LLM provider named — Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, AWS Bedrock — none disclosed.
  • "Full MCP support" — claimed but not documented. No published MCP server name, no tool list, no permission model, no public registry comparable to Workday ASOR or Stratum's four servers.
Eglin's read The honest classification: Windmill is shipping an evidence-aggregation surface for performance reviews with an LLM chat layer on top, branded as a "context graph." That is a real product with credible early traction — 100+ customers in seven months is not nothing — and it solves a known pain point (managers writing reviews from a blank page). It is not a substrate in the People Graph 360 §I sense. The "graph" is the noun the company uses to describe the relationship between an insight and its citation, not the noun for a persistent property graph. Two of the three load-bearing substrate properties (time-as-edge, per-edge consent) are not just unshipped — they are unaddressed in public material. The third (cross-system identity resolution) is implied by the integration surface but not described as architectural commitment.

The Yext-lineage observation. Both Shaw and Distelburger come from Yext, which built the "Answers Platform" / public-facts knowledge graph for business locations and entities. That background means the team has real schema-and-entity-resolution chops — but their prior product was a graph over public business data, not over workforce-private data with consent, retention, and identity-resolution obligations attached. The instinct to call this a "graph" is consistent with the Yext design DNA. Whether that DNA scales into the consent-bound, time-aware shape an HR substrate requires is the open question, and one their public material does not engage with at the time of this writing.

§2 · The four-layer model, evaluated

What each layer would have to become.

Windmill names four layers: People, Evidence, Expectations, Perspectives. For each, this section names (a) what they are shipping today, (b) what they would have to ship for that layer to threaten Stratum's substrate claim, and (c) confidence that they will. The synthesis sits at the end.

Layer 1 · People

"Who they are and how they connect."

Shipping today
A roster of employees, drawn from connected sources (Slack handles, GitHub committers, Google Workspace accounts). Reporting structure inferred from organizational chart inputs. No public description of cross-system entity resolution; identity appears to be tenant-scoped and account-keyed within each integrated tool.
Substrate threshold
A canonical worker identity resolved across Slack handle ≠ GitHub committer ≠ Google account ≠ HRIS record ≠ payroll ID, governed by a published reconciliation algorithm with confidence scores, hold-and-review queues for ambiguous matches, and an audit trail per merge. A versioned, time-stamped identity graph queryable by external systems via published schema. This is the load-bearing distinction.
Confidence they ship this
Low. No public material engages with identity resolution as an architectural problem. The Yext lineage suggests the team could build it, but the product is currently scoped to performance reviews — a domain where soft identity matching (Slack handle ≈ Google account by email) is sufficient. Building hard identity resolution would require an architectural pivot the seed funding does not obviously support.
Layer 2 · Evidence

"What actually happened."

Shipping today
Ingestion and indexing of artifacts across Slack threads, GitHub PRs and commits, Google Docs, calendar entries, and 30+ named integrations. Citation-grounded retrieval: each LLM-generated insight in a draft review traces back to a specific source artifact. This is the strongest publicly verified capability and the one that explains the early customer traction.
Substrate threshold
A persistent, time-versioned evidence store with provenance and consent metadata on every edge, queryable independently of the chat surface, with retention policy expressed as graph property and per-evidence-class deletion APIs that satisfy GDPR Article 17 and CPRA at the storage layer. A published schema for what evidence categories the system stores and what it does not. This is a serious infrastructure build.
Confidence they ship this
Low-medium. The evidence-aggregation surface is the product. The substrate-shaped version of it is an engineering investment that pushes the product further from its current "drafted review" UX surface — a roadmap divergence the team has not signaled.
Layer 3 · Expectations

"What good looks like."

Shipping today
Per-role calibration patterns derived from accumulated review and feedback content within a customer tenant — the kind of "your strong-performer engineers ship X PRs/quarter with Y review velocity" inference a tenant-bound evidence store enables. Not a cross-customer benchmarking corpus (no public claim of that scale).
Substrate threshold
A skills-and-expectations ontology with cross-tenant calibration, an explicit fairness / bias-audit posture for any inference that flows into a hiring or promotion decision (Mobley v. Workday read-across applies the moment an evidence platform's inferences inform employment outcomes), and a published taxonomy comparable to Cornerstone's 55,000-skill claim or Eightfold's skills graph. None of this is in public material.
Confidence they ship this
Low. This is where Windmill collides with the existing graph-as-recommendation cluster (Eightfold, Cornerstone, Gloat) and where it would face L&D and compliance incumbents with twenty-year head starts. Strategically unlikely from a $12M seed.
Layer 4 · Perspectives

"What people think."

Shipping today
Pulse surveys, 1:1 notes, continuous feedback capture, manager-and-peer review inputs. A standard performance-and-engagement input surface for a startup at this stage. Culture Amp, 15Five, and Lattice all ship comparable capability at much larger customer scale.
Substrate threshold
Sentiment and perspective as queryable, consent-bound, time-versioned graph properties — not just survey results, but a longitudinal employee-voice graph with explicit anonymity-vs-attribution policy at the edge level. Plus integration into the broader workforce-decision flow such that perspective data is structurally inseparable from employment-outcome decisions, governed by an audit posture comparable to Culture Amp's 1B+ benchmarking corpus.
Confidence they ship this
Low. The engagement-survey category has incumbents whose data corpora are not catchable by a seed-stage startup. The substrate version of this layer is a Culture Amp / Lattice problem space, and Windmill is too early to engage it.
Synthesis For Windmill to threaten Stratum's substrate claim, they would have to ship (X) hard cross-system identity resolution with a published reconciliation algorithm and audit trail, (Y) a persistent time-versioned graph store with consent metadata on every edge and per-evidence-class deletion APIs, and (Z) an explicit governance posture — third-party bias audit, named LLM provider, model cards — once their inferences begin to influence employment outcomes. The team shape they raised on does not suggest they intend to. A $12M seed for a performance-review product with three product-marketing-shaped founders (none of whom is a graph-database architect on the public record) builds the evidence-aggregation surface they have shipped, not the substrate. Substrate-threat probability in a 6–12 month window: ~12–15%. Positioning-threat probability — "context graph" / "workforce graph" appearing in trade press as a category-defining noun for what Stratum claims architecturally — is materially higher and is what this battlecard is sized to track.
§3 · Escalation milestones

Two falsifiable triggers. Watched on cadence.

These are the two events that move Windmill from L2 Watch to L3 Action and convert this partial battlecard into a full one. Both are public-signal triggers — they require no private intelligence to detect. The monitoring assignments below name who watches what, on what cadence, with what notification.

Trigger A · Series A announcement with substrate language

If the Series A press release uses the following vocabulary, escalate.

The falsifiable language: "system of record," "graph database," "identity resolution across systems," or any framing that asserts architectural depth beyond the current evidence-surface description. Any one of these phrases appearing in Windmill's own Series A copy — not analyst paraphrase — fires the trigger. Tier-A trade-press paraphrase ("Windmill is positioning its context graph as the system of record for workforce decisions…") also fires the trigger if the paraphrase is attributable to Windmill leadership in the article.

Why this matters: a Series A typically arrives 12–24 months after seed. Inspired Capital's portfolio companies historically announce A rounds in the $30–60M range and the messaging often expands beyond the seed-stage product framing. If Windmill chooses substrate vocabulary at A, they are committing to a roadmap the seed-stage product has not yet built — but they are also claiming the category-noun publicly. That noun overlap with Stratum's people-graph positioning is the editorial risk.

Watcher: Eglin · Cadence: weekly funding-news sweep + on-event alert · Notify: Helm and Compass within 24h of detection · Action: full battlecard within 7 days, plus a brief to the Compact.

Trigger B · Named reference customer using graph vocabulary

If a published customer case study uses graph vocabulary to describe Windmill, escalate.

The falsifiable language: a Windmill customer (not Windmill itself) describing what Windmill does for them using nouns like "graph," "substrate," "system of record," or "single source of truth" — in a published case study, conference talk, podcast appearance, or written testimonial that is attributable to the customer's HR or technology leadership. Marketing copy on the Windmill site repeating their own framing does not count; the trigger requires customer adoption of the vocabulary, which is the signal that the category-noun is travelling.

Why this matters: vocabulary captured at the customer layer is materially harder to reframe than vocabulary at the vendor layer. If a Kalshi or Rho or Merge spokesperson describes Windmill as "our workforce graph," that quote will be reused by analysts, journalists, and competing vendors. The Cornerstone battlecard documents the same dynamic for "people graph" at the incumbent layer. Windmill at the startup layer is a different shape of the same risk.

Watcher: Eglin · Cadence: monthly customer-content sweep (Windmill blog, customer LinkedIn, podcast appearances by named customers) + Tier-A analyst publications (Bersin, Sapient Insights, Brian Sommer) · Notify: Helm and Compass within 24h · Action: full battlecard within 7 days, plus an updated language map for the Compass buyer-facing competitive page.

Monitoring cadence (consolidated) Weekly funding-news sweep includes Inspired Capital portfolio activity and Windmill's own press surface (PR Newswire, BusinessWire, company blog). Monthly customer-content sweep includes Windmill's published case studies, customer LinkedIn posts mentioning Windmill, and HCM-analyst publications scanning for "context graph" or "workforce graph" as a category noun. Both sweeps roll into Eglin's weekly digest, with the Windmill row carrying a status line: "no triggers fired" or "Trigger [A/B] fired on [date]." If 90 days pass with no triggers fired and no Series A signal, Eglin will reassess the threat scoring and either downgrade Windmill from L2 Watch or maintain at L2 with revised expected-trigger dates.

What I don't have. Next refresh triggers. Beyond the two escalation triggers in §3: (1) a Windmill engineering blog post describing the storage layer; (2) any independent technical review on Hacker News, /r/ExperiencedDevs, or a credentialled engineer's Substack assessing the architecture; (3) Series A timing signals (typical Inspired A-round lag is 12–18 months from seed, putting an A announcement window in late Q1 2027 unless accelerated); (4) any addition of an architecture-named hire (head of data, head of platform, VP engineering with a graph-database background) signalling a roadmap pivot toward substrate-shape.

O-2 self-check. This battlecard advances O-2 in three concrete ways. (a) It builds the always-on signal stream into the partial-battlecard format — a smaller, faster artifact for a smaller, faster threat — which is exactly the kind of forward-leaning, proportional research O-2 calls for. (b) It separates marketing vocabulary ("context graph") from architectural reality (evidence aggregation + LLM chat) at the level of granularity the Compact needs to make a positioning call without re-doing the source-reading. (c) It names two falsifiable, public-signal escalation triggers with explicit watcher / cadence / notification — closed-loop monitoring rather than open-ended "we'll keep an eye on them." The honest take: Windmill is a credible early-stage product run by experienced founders with a positioning-vocabulary collision that matters and an architecture story that does not yet justify the noun. Stratum's response is not defensive — it is to keep publishing depth, watch the two triggers, and reassess if either fires.
