From signal to docket. The California-nexus theory survived.

Per HR Dive's 24 June piece "Workday can't shake California AI discrimination claims" [1], Bloomberg Law's "Workday Loses Bid to Toss AI Discrimination Suit in California" [2], and Duane Morris' 24 June class-action defense post [3], Judge Rita F. Lin of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California issued an order on 22 June 2026 denying in part and granting in part Workday's motion to dismiss the third amended complaint. Fact: the FEHA claims survive — Lin held that plaintiffs "plausibly alleged enough of a California nexus" because Workday "designs, operates and controls" its algorithmic screening tools from its California headquarters, which is sufficient to apply California's Fair Employment and Housing Act to applicants outside California. Fact: the ADA claim survives for one named plaintiff and similarly-situated others on a proxy-indicator theory tying rejections to medical-related-leave screening, asthma, and cancer-survivorship signals. Fact: Lin granted dismissal of a race-based disparate-impact claim added in the third amended complaint as "unauthorized" (procedural rather than substantive grounds) and dismissed the theory that Workday is liable as an employer for using the screening procedures in its own hiring. Inference (labeled): the surviving FEHA California-nexus theory is the operative finding for HR-tech vendors — the holding establishes that a vendor's California domicile is enough to subject its nationwide AI-screening output to a California-law private right of action; the procedural posture is now discovery, not pleading, so the substantive bias-audit question moves from "is this case viable" to "what does the discovery record show."
